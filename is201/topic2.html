IS 201 · Chapter 1 — Foundations and Ethical Reasoning

Topic 2 — Ethical Frameworks and Professional Responsibility

A guided reading on the four major ways of reasoning about ethical problems in computing, why frameworks alone can't hand us automatic answers, and how professional codes turn ethical values into standards of practice.

Faculty-in-Charge: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Ethical Lenses — Why Ethics Matters

Why Ethics Matters in Computing

Computing is not only a technical activity. The technologies we design, develop, deploy, and use can affect people's privacy, safety, opportunities, relationships, work, and access to information and services.

Consider the decisions computing professionals routinely make. What information should an application collect? When is a system safe enough to deploy? Should an organization use AI to make decisions about people? What should a developer do after discovering a serious security vulnerability? Who is responsible when an automated system causes harm?

Technical questions ask: Can we build it? Does it work?

Ethical questions go further: Should we build it? Should we use it this way? Who could benefit or be harmed? What responsibilities do we have?

As computing becomes increasingly embedded in society, technical competence must therefore be accompanied by ethical judgment and professional responsibility.

There is no single ethical framework that automatically resolves every difficult situation. Instead, different approaches direct our attention to different aspects of a problem. Three major traditions in normative ethics are commonly identified as consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics. Questions of justice and fairness provide another particularly important lens for examining how technologies distribute benefits, risks, opportunities, and burdens across society.

A simple way to distinguish the four lenses
  • Consequences — What will happen overall?
  • Duties and Rights — What are we obligated to do?
  • Virtue and Character — What kind of professional should I be?
  • Justice and Fairness — How are benefits and burdens distributed?
02 · Ethical Lenses — Consequences

Consequences: What Will Happen Overall?

Consequentialist ethics evaluates actions primarily in terms of their outcomes. A familiar example is utilitarianism, which asks us to consider the overall benefits and harms resulting from an action.

In computing, this might mean asking:

  • What are the likely benefits and harms?
  • How serious and likely are these outcomes?
  • What unintended consequences might occur?
  • Which option is likely to produce the best overall outcome?

Suppose a university introduces an AI system that identifies students considered at risk of dropping out. If the system allows earlier intervention and helps many students succeed, that is an important benefit. But if inaccurate predictions cause harm to some students, those consequences must also be considered. A consequences-based analysis therefore asks us to look beyond whether a technology works and examine what happens because it is used.

03 · Ethical Lenses — Duties & Rights

Duties and Rights: What Are We Obligated to Do?

Deontological ethics emphasizes duties, obligations, rules, and rights rather than judging an action solely by its consequences. From this perspective, some actions may be wrong even if they could produce beneficial results.

For computing professionals, relevant questions might include:

  • What duties do I have toward users?
  • What rights must be respected?
  • Are people's privacy and autonomy being protected?
  • Have people given meaningful consent?
  • Am I being honest about what the system can and cannot do?
  • Are there actions I should refuse even if they would benefit my organization?

An organization, for example, might benefit from secretly collecting extensive information about its users. Even if the data improves its services, we must still ask whether collecting that information without meaningful consent respects users' rights. Good consequences do not necessarily justify every means used to achieve them.

Quick check

2. A company argues that because its data collection technically complies with its posted terms of service, it has no further ethical obligation to users. From a duties-and-rights perspective, what is the strongest objection to that argument?

04 · Ethical Lenses — Virtue & Character

Virtue and Character: What Kind of Professional Should I Be?

Virtue ethics shifts attention from rules and consequences toward moral character, with roots in the work of Aristotle. Instead of asking only "What should I do?", it also asks what qualities a good person or professional should develop.

In computing, we might ask:

  • What would an honest professional do?
  • What would responsibility require?
  • Am I demonstrating integrity?
  • Do I have the courage to raise a concern when something is wrong?
  • Am I exercising appropriate care and competence in my work?

Imagine discovering a serious defect shortly before a major software release. Reporting it may delay the project and upset your organization or client. Virtue ethics asks whether remaining silent would be consistent with being an honest, responsible, and trustworthy professional. Technical expertise tells us what we can do. Character influences how responsibly we use that expertise.

Quick check

3. Virtue ethics asks what a responsible, honest professional would do, rather than only "What does the rule require?" or "What produces the best outcome?" Which scenario best illustrates that distinct focus?

05 · Ethical Lenses — Justice & Fairness

Justice and Fairness: How Are Benefits and Burdens Distributed?

Justice and fairness focus not simply on the total benefits and harms produced by a technology, but on how those benefits, opportunities, risks, and burdens are distributed among people and groups.

In computing, we might ask:

  • Are benefits and burdens distributed fairly?
  • Are some people or groups disproportionately disadvantaged?
  • Are similar cases treated consistently?
  • Does everyone have a fair opportunity to benefit?
  • Are vulnerable or marginalized groups placed at greater risk?

Consider an AI recruitment system that performs well overall but consistently disadvantages applicants from a particular demographic group. A consequences-based analysis might examine the system's overall benefits and harms. A justice-based analysis asks a different question: are particular people or groups unfairly bearing more of the harm? A technology can therefore produce positive outcomes overall while still raising serious concerns about fairness.

This becomes especially important when computing systems influence access to education, employment, healthcare, financial services, government services, or other significant opportunities.

06 · Putting Ethics to Work — Worked Example

Apply the Four Lenses: Should We Deploy?

A university ICT office is preparing to deploy a new system needed for an important university activity. During final testing, the development team discovers a defect affecting a small percentage of transactions.

Delaying deployment would disrupt university operations and require hundreds of transactions to be processed manually. Deploying immediately would avoid this disruption, but some users could receive incorrect records.

The requesting office asks the ICT team to deploy the system and fix the defect afterward.

What should the ICT team do?

Examine the situation using the four ethical lenses:

  • Consequences — What are the likely overall benefits and harms of deploying now compared with delaying deployment?
  • Duties and Rights — What obligations does the ICT team have to users, the requesting office, and the university? What rights or interests must be respected?
  • Virtue and Character — What would a competent, honest, prudent, and responsible ICT professional do?
  • Justice and Fairness — Who receives the benefits of immediate deployment, and who bears the risks? Is it fair for a smaller group of users to bear the risk so that the majority can avoid disruption?

You may find that the four lenses highlight different aspects of the problem. This illustrates an important point: ethical frameworks help us reason about difficult decisions, but they do not necessarily produce a single, automatic answer.

07 · Putting Ethics to Work — No Automatic Answers

Ethical Frameworks Do Not Give Automatic Answers

Consider again the AI system designed to identify students at risk of dropping out. A consequences-based approach might examine whether the system ultimately helps more students than it harms. A duties-and-rights approach might focus on students' privacy, autonomy, and appropriate use of their personal information. A virtue-based approach might ask what responsible and trustworthy educators, administrators, and developers should do. A justice-based approach might investigate whether the system performs differently across socioeconomic, gender, geographic, or other groups.

Now consider a different situation: a software developer discovers a serious security vulnerability in an organization's system. Fixing it immediately would require taking the system offline for several hours, disrupting services for thousands of users. Leaving it online avoids the disruption but risks exploitation by attackers.

LensHow it examines the vulnerability dilemma
ConsequencesCompares the harm of temporarily disrupting the service with the potentially much greater harm of a security breach.
Duties & rightsEmphasizes the organization's duty to protect users and safeguard their personal information, even when inconvenient or costly.
Virtue & characterAsks what an honest, prudent, and responsible computing professional would do after discovering such a risk.
Justice & fairnessConsiders who would be most affected either way — would offline time disproportionately hurt users who depend most on the service, or would a breach expose some groups to greater harm?

The same situation can therefore look different depending on the ethical lens we use. These perspectives may highlight different concerns and sometimes point toward different conclusions — which is precisely why ethical reasoning matters.

The purpose of an ethical framework is not to replace judgment with a formula. It gives us a structured way to identify what matters, examine competing considerations, and justify our decisions.

08 · Putting Ethics to Work — Ethics Across IT

Ethics Across Information Technology

Ethical issues are not confined to one particular area of computing. They arise throughout the development, use, and management of information technology, involving areas such as privacy, cybersecurity, intellectual property, artificial intelligence, automation, and professional conduct.

Data Privacy

Organizations collect enormous amounts of information about individuals. Some of this information may be necessary to provide digital services, but the ability to collect data does not automatically justify its collection or use. Computing professionals should ask: What information is actually necessary? Do users understand what is being collected? How will it be used? Who will have access to it? How should it be protected? Privacy is therefore not merely a matter of securing databases — it also concerns whether information should have been collected, used, or shared in the first place.

Cybersecurity

Professionals responsible for information systems may encounter vulnerabilities, security incidents, unauthorized access, or weaknesses that could place users at risk. Technical knowledge gives professionals the ability to identify and address these problems, but it also creates responsibilities regarding how that knowledge is used. Discovering a vulnerability raises questions about disclosure, confidentiality, organizational responsibility, and the protection of affected users. Security is therefore not simply a technical objective — protecting people from foreseeable digital harm is also an ethical responsibility.

Intellectual Property

Digital technologies make software, information, images, music, research, and other creative works extremely easy to reproduce and distribute. The fact that something can be copied does not necessarily mean that it should be copied. Computing professionals must consider questions of ownership, attribution, licensing, permission, and appropriate use — issues that become increasingly complicated when software is assembled from multiple libraries and repositories, or when generative AI systems produce content based on large collections of existing works.

Artificial Intelligence and Automation

AI and automation can improve efficiency, expand access to services, support decision-making, and perform tasks at scales that would otherwise be difficult. At the same time, they can introduce concerns involving bias, privacy, transparency, accountability, employment, misinformation, and human autonomy. The important ethical question is therefore not simply whether AI can perform a particular task — we must also ask when its use is appropriate, what safeguards are necessary, and who remains accountable for its consequences.

09 · Professional Codes — From Ethics to Responsibility

From Ethics to Professional Responsibility

Ethical responsibility becomes especially important when someone possesses specialized knowledge or professional authority. Computing professionals often make decisions that affect people who cannot inspect the source code, understand the algorithms, evaluate the security architecture, or independently determine whether a system is safe.

This creates professional responsibility: recognizing that technical expertise comes with obligations toward clients, employers, colleagues, users, the profession, and society. This is one reason professional organizations develop codes of ethics — codes that translate broader ethical values into principles and responsibilities relevant to professional practice.

Ethical frameworks help us reason about what ought to be done. Professional codes translate ethical values into expectations for people who practice computing.

Two important examples are the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and the IEEE-CS/ACM Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice. The ACM Code addresses computing professionals broadly, while the Software Engineering Code focuses specifically on ethical responsibilities arising from the development and maintenance of software.

10 · Professional Codes — ACM Code of Ethics

The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct provides ethical guidance for computing professionals. At its foundation is the idea that computing professionals should use their skills for the benefit of society.

Its general ethical principles call on computing professionals to:

  1. Contribute to society and human well-being.
  2. Avoid harm.
  3. Be honest and trustworthy.
  4. Be fair and take action not to discriminate.
  5. Respect the work required to produce new ideas, inventions, creative works, and computing artifacts.
  6. Respect privacy.
  7. Honor confidentiality.

The Code goes beyond simply asking whether something is technically possible or legally permissible. Imagine that an application collects considerably more personal information than its users realize. The collection might technically comply with an organization's policies or terms of service — but professional ethics requires additional questions: Is collecting all this information necessary? Are users sufficiently informed? Could the information be misused? Are we respecting their privacy?

Key distinction. Something can be legal without necessarily being ethical.

The ACM Code also addresses more specific professional responsibilities, including maintaining professional competence, producing high-quality work, evaluating systems and their risks, respecting applicable rules, and designing systems that are secure and usable. Professional ethics therefore involves not only avoiding obvious wrongdoing, but also exercising care, competence, judgment, and responsibility in one's work.

11 · Professional Codes — Software Engineering Code

The IEEE-CS/ACM Software Engineering Code

Software engineers have particular responsibilities because software increasingly forms part of the infrastructure on which individuals, organizations, and societies depend. The IEEE-CS/ACM Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice organizes these responsibilities around eight principles:

Public· Client & Employer· Product· Judgment· Management· Profession· Colleagues· Self
  • Public — Software engineers should act consistently with the public interest.
  • Client and Employer — They should act in the best interests of their clients and employers, consistent with the public interest.
  • Product — Software and related modifications should meet the highest professional standards possible.
  • Judgment — Software engineers should maintain integrity and independence in their professional judgment.
  • Management — Managers and leaders should promote ethical approaches to software development and maintenance.
  • Profession — Software engineers should advance the integrity and reputation of the profession.
  • Colleagues — Professionals should be fair to and supportive of their colleagues.
  • Self — Software engineers should continue developing their professional competence and promote ethical practice.

One idea is particularly important throughout the Code: the public interest. Suppose your organization wants a system deployed immediately, testing is incomplete, and you know that an unresolved defect could expose users' personal information. It is not sufficient to say, "My supervisor told me to deploy it." Professional responsibility requires considering the people who may be affected by that decision — following instructions does not eliminate professional accountability.

12 · Professional Codes — When Responsibilities Conflict

When Professional Responsibilities Conflict

Ethical problems become particularly difficult when legitimate responsibilities point in different directions. Imagine you discover a serious security vulnerability in software developed by your organization. Your supervisor tells you not to disclose the problem because doing so could damage the organization's reputation.

You now have several responsibilities:

  • To your employer — protect legitimate organizational interests and confidential information.
  • To users — protect them from foreseeable harm.
  • To the public — consider the wider consequences of an insecure system.
  • To your profession — maintain honesty, competence, and integrity.

What should take priority? The IEEE-CS/ACM Code provides an important principle: responsibilities toward clients and employers must remain consistent with the public interest.

Professionalism does not mean unquestioning obedience. Depending on the circumstances, responsible professional conduct may require raising concerns, documenting risks, escalating problems, recommending alternatives, or refusing to participate in an action that would violate serious professional obligations.

Professional responsibility sometimes means making difficult decisions precisely because different responsibilities are legitimate but cannot all be satisfied equally.

13 · Decision & Takeaway — Ethical Decision-Making

Putting It All Together: Ethical Decision-Making

Professional codes are valuable, but they cannot anticipate every possible situation. Ethical frameworks and professional codes should guide professional judgment rather than replace it.

A checklist might tell you whether software testing was performed — ethical reasoning asks whether the testing was sufficient given the potential consequences of failure. A policy might tell you that collecting certain information is permitted — ethical reasoning asks whether collecting it is necessary, proportionate, transparent, and respectful of the people involved. A manager might authorize deployment — ethical reasoning still asks whether deployment is responsible.

When confronted with an ethical problem in computing, consider the following questions:

  1. What are the facts? Understand what is actually happening before making a judgment.
  2. Who are the stakeholders? Identify the people and groups who may be directly or indirectly affected.
  3. What are the possible consequences? Consider potential benefits, harms, risks, and unintended effects.
  4. What duties and rights are involved? Consider privacy, autonomy, honesty, confidentiality, safety, and other obligations.
  5. What would responsible professional character require? Consider integrity, honesty, competence, fairness, and courage.
  6. Is the situation fair? Examine how benefits, opportunities, risks, and burdens are distributed.
  7. What do relevant professional codes require? Apply principles from codes such as those of ACM and IEEE-CS/ACM.
  8. What alternatives are available? Ethical choices are rarely limited to only two options.
  9. Can you justify your decision? A professional should be able to explain why a decision is ethically defensible.

This process brings together the ideas in this primer: ethical frameworks help us examine the problem from different perspectives, professional codes identify responsibilities associated with computing practice, and professional judgment requires us to consider both and make a decision we can reasonably justify.

14 · Decision & Takeaway — Key Idea

Key Idea

Ethical frameworks help us examine decisions from different angles — they don't replace professional judgment.

Computing professionals make decisions that can affect privacy, safety, fairness, opportunity, access, and human well-being. Ethical frameworks help us examine those decisions from different perspectives. Professional codes translate ethical principles into responsibilities for computing practice.

Computing professionals must ask
  • Can we build it?
  • Does it work?
  • Is it legal?
They must also ask
  • Should we build it?
  • How should we build it?
  • Who might be affected?
  • What responsibilities do we have?

The goal is not to memorize rules or find a formula that automatically produces the "ethical" answer. It is to develop the ability to recognize ethical problems, examine them from multiple perspectives, exercise professional judgment, and justify responsible decisions.
