Topic 4 — Cybersecurity and Cyberethics

A guided reading on cybersecurity as an ethical responsibility — the CIA triad, risk and duty of care, cybercrime under RA 10175, the human element, and the professional responsibilities that come with privileged access.

Faculty-in-Charge: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Introduction

Beyond Technical Protection

Digital systems have become essential to communication, education, government, commerce, banking, healthcare, and everyday life.

As society becomes increasingly dependent on these systems, protecting them from unauthorized access, disruption, manipulation, and misuse becomes more than a technical concern. It becomes an ethical responsibility.

Cybersecurity is concerned with protecting systems, networks, devices, and information from threats and attacks. Cyberethics asks broader questions about what people and organizations ought to do in digital environments:

  • Who is responsible when inadequate security causes harm?
  • How much monitoring is justified in the name of security?
  • When does accessing a system cross an ethical boundary?
  • What responsibilities do information systems professionals have when they discover vulnerabilities or security incidents?

These questions matter because cybersecurity decisions affect not only computers and data, but also people, organizations, rights, and society.

The central question

Cybersecurity asks how systems can be protected. Cyberethics also asks what we ought to do — and what we ought not to do — with the power to access, monitor, and control them.

02 · Introduction

Why This Matters to Information Systems Students and Professionals

Information systems professionals operate at the intersection of people, organizations, data, processes, and technology. Cybersecurity decisions are therefore part of ordinary IS practice, not merely the responsibility of specialized cybersecurity professionals.

An IS professional may help determine:

  • who receives access to a system;
  • what activities are logged;
  • how information is protected;
  • how security incidents are handled;
  • whether vulnerable systems remain in operation; and
  • how users are informed about risks.

These decisions can affect privacy, organizational trust, access to services, financial resources, and people's rights and well-being.

Cybersecurity competence involves more than knowing how to protect a system technically. It also requires recognizing who may be harmed, what responsibilities are involved, what level of security is reasonable and proportionate, and what ethical boundaries should not be crossed in the pursuit of security.

03 · Cybersecurity and Ethical Responsibility

Cybersecurity as an Ethical Concern

Cybersecurity is often understood primarily as a technical problem: protecting systems, networks, devices, and data from unauthorized access, disruption, manipulation, or destruction. Technical measures such as authentication, access controls, encryption, software updates, backups, and network monitoring are essential. However, cybersecurity is also fundamentally an ethical concern because security decisions can directly affect people, organizations, and society.

Security Failures Cause Real Harm

A security failure can cause more than technical inconvenience:

If this is compromised…The harm may include…
A university systemExposure of student records or interruption of essential academic services
A financial systemFinancial loss or identity theft
A hospital (via ransomware)Interference with access to critical information and services

In each case, weaknesses in cybersecurity can translate into real harm to individuals and communities.

Responsibility and Power

Cybersecurity also involves decisions about responsibility and power. Organizations decide how much security to provide, what risks are acceptable, who should have access to information, and how users should be monitored. IS and IT professionals may possess privileged access to databases, servers, accounts, logs, and other resources. The ability to access or control these resources does not automatically justify every possible use of that capability.

For example, a system administrator may technically be able to view confidential employee or student records while maintaining a system.

Two different questions

A technical question
  • Can the administrator access the information?
An ethical question
  • Should the administrator access it — under what circumstances, and for what purpose?

Cybersecurity therefore concerns not only how systems should be protected, but also who has responsibilities toward those systems and the people who depend on them. It requires professionals and organizations to consider potential harm, respect privacy and confidentiality, exercise appropriate care, and use their technical capabilities responsibly.

The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct reflects this broader responsibility by requiring computing professionals to avoid harm, respect privacy, honor confidentiality, maintain professional competence, and design and implement systems that are robustly and usably secure.

A useful principle — Cybersecurity protects systems, but the ultimate reason for protecting systems is to protect the people and activities that depend on them.

04 · Cybersecurity and Ethical Responsibility

What Are We Protecting? The CIA Triad

A foundational model in cybersecurity is the CIA triad, which identifies three fundamental security objectives: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability. These principles help explain what cybersecurity seeks to protect and what can be harmed when security fails.

Confidentiality+ Integrity+ Availability

Confidentiality

Confidentiality — ensuring that information is accessible only to people, systems, or processes that are authorized to access it. It protects information from unauthorized disclosure.

Confidentiality is particularly important when systems contain sensitive information such as personal data, financial records, student records, health information, passwords, or confidential organizational documents. Common measures include authentication, access controls, encryption, and appropriate handling of sensitive information.

For example, a university employee may legitimately have access to student records because that access is necessary for their work. Another employee without such responsibilities should not be able to view the same records simply out of curiosity. If student information is accessed or disclosed without authorization, confidentiality has been compromised.

From an ethical perspective, confidentiality involves trust and respect for the interests and rights of others. Individuals and organizations entrust information to information systems with the expectation that it will not be accessed or disclosed without legitimate justification.

Integrity

Integrity — protecting information and systems from unauthorized or improper modification, deletion, or manipulation. Information should remain accurate, complete, and trustworthy.

Integrity is essential because organizations increasingly rely on digital information to make decisions. Even if information remains confidential, it may become harmful or useless if it has been altered improperly.

If an unauthorized person changes a student's grade, modifies enrollment information, or alters financial records, the primary problem is not necessarily that confidential information was exposed. The problem is that the trustworthiness of the information has been compromised.

Integrity also extends beyond deliberate attacks. Accidental changes, software errors, incorrect system configurations, and poorly designed processes can also undermine the accuracy and reliability of information. Measures such as access controls, audit logs, validation mechanisms, version control, and backups can help preserve integrity.

Ethically, protecting integrity means recognizing that people and organizations make important decisions based on information systems. Professionals therefore have a responsibility to help ensure that information remains accurate, reliable, and protected against improper alteration.

Availability

Availability — ensuring that authorized users can access information, systems, and services when they legitimately need them.

A system may successfully protect confidentiality and integrity but still fail its users if it is unavailable when needed. Availability can be affected by hardware failures, software problems, network outages, denial-of-service attacks, ransomware, natural disasters, or inadequate infrastructure.

If a university's enrollment system becomes inaccessible during the enrollment period, students may be unable to register for courses. If a hospital information system becomes unavailable during an emergency, the consequences may be considerably more serious. Organizations protect availability through backups, redundancy, system monitoring, disaster recovery, regular maintenance, and appropriate incident-response procedures.

From an ethical perspective, availability matters because people increasingly depend on digital systems to exercise rights, fulfill responsibilities, and access important services. The greater the importance of a system, the greater the potential harm caused by prolonged or preventable disruption.

The CIA Triad in Practice

The three objectives are closely related, but a cybersecurity incident may affect one, two, or all three.

IncidentPrimary Security Concern
Someone views confidential student records without authorizationConfidentiality
Someone changes a student's grade without authorizationIntegrity
Students cannot access the system because of a cyberattackAvailability
Ransomware prevents access to records and threatens to publish stolen dataConfidentiality, Integrity, and Availability

The CIA triad shifts the question from simply "Is the system secure?" to more specific questions:

  • Is information protected from unauthorized disclosure?
  • Can we trust that it has not been improperly altered?
  • Can legitimate users access the system when they need it?

For cyberethics, these questions matter because failures of confidentiality, integrity, and availability ultimately affect people:

Failure of…May lead to…
ConfidentialityViolations of privacy
IntegrityUnfair or incorrect decisions
AvailabilityPeople being unable to access essential services

Thus, the CIA triad is not only a technical model for information security. It also helps identify what interests may be harmed when cybersecurity fails and why protecting information systems carries ethical responsibilities.

05 · Cybersecurity and Ethical Responsibility

Risk, Harm, and Responsibility

Perfect security does not exist. Organizations therefore make decisions about risk: what could go wrong, how likely it is to happen, how serious the consequences could be, and what protections are reasonable.

Scenario — An organization knows that an outdated system contains serious vulnerabilities but postpones an upgrade because replacement is expensive. Months later, attackers exploit the vulnerability and obtain thousands of users' records.

The attackers are responsible for the attack. But does responsibility end there?

The organization may also bear responsibility if it knew about a significant risk and failed to take reasonable precautions. Cybersecurity therefore involves the idea of duty of care.

Duty of care — the responsibility to take reasonable measures to prevent foreseeable harm.

This does not mean that every successful cyberattack proves negligence. Even well-protected systems can be compromised. Ethical evaluation should consider:

  1. What risks were known?
  2. What protections were reasonably available?
  3. What responsibilities did the organization have?
  4. Were appropriate precautions actually taken?

06 · Cybersecurity and Ethical Responsibility

Security: Not the Only Value

Greater security is not automatically better in every circumstance.

Organizations might improve security by collecting more information, monitoring more activities, restricting more access, requiring additional authentication, or recording detailed user behavior. These measures may reduce certain risks, but they can also affect privacy, autonomy, accessibility, usability, and trust.

Consider employee monitoring. Logging suspicious network activity may be a reasonable cybersecurity measure. Recording every website visited, message sent, and keystroke typed represents a substantially greater degree of intrusion.

Asking the right question

Not only
  • Does this make the system more secure?
But also
  • Is the security measure necessary, proportionate, transparent, and respectful of the people affected by it?

This connects cyberethics directly with the privacy and digital rights issues discussed in Topic 3.

07 · Cybercrime and Digital Harm

Cyber Threats

Cyber threats are actions or events that can compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of digital systems and information. While cyberattacks take place through computers and networks, their consequences extend well beyond technology.

They can result in financial loss, privacy violations, identity theft, reputational damage, disruption of essential services, and harm to individuals and organizations.

Common Cyber Threats

ThreatWhat it does
PhishingUses deceptive messages to obtain sensitive information or persuade users to perform harmful actions
MalwareMalicious software designed to damage, disrupt, spy on, or gain unauthorized access to systems
RansomwareTypically prevents access to systems or data and demands payment for their restoration
Social engineeringTargets people rather than purely technical vulnerabilities, manipulating trust, authority, fear, urgency, or curiosity to gain information or access

Cyber threats can also involve unauthorized access, data theft or manipulation, disruption of systems, and misuse of legitimate accounts or privileges. Importantly, attacks do not always require sophisticated technical methods. A stolen password, a convincing phishing message, or misuse of authorized access can be enough to compromise an information system.

Severity Depends on the Target

Disrupting an entertainment website is different from disrupting a hospital, banking system, government service, or university information system. The same type of attack can therefore have very different ethical consequences depending on who is affected, what information or services are involved, and how serious the resulting harm may be.

The cyberethics concern — not merely how an attack works, but what harm it causes, whose rights or interests are affected, and who bears responsibility for preventing and responding to that harm.

08 · Cybercrime and Digital Harm

The Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012

In the Philippines, an important legal foundation for understanding cybercrime is Republic Act No. 10175, or the Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012.

The law identifies several categories of cybercrime offenses. These include offenses against the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of computer data and systems:

OffenseWhat it involves
Illegal accessAccessing the whole or any part of a computer system without right
Illegal interceptionUnauthorized interception of non-public computer data transmissions
Data interferenceUnauthorized alteration, damage, deletion, or deterioration of computer data
System interferenceUnauthorized hindering or interference with the functioning of computer systems or networks
Misuse of devicesCertain devices, programs, passwords, or access credentials intended or adapted for committing cybercrime
CybersquattingCertain bad-faith acquisition of domain names

RA 10175 also addresses specified computer-related offenses, including computer-related forgery, fraud, and identity theft, as well as certain content-related offenses.

Connecting the Law to the CIA Triad

OffenseThreatens
Illegal access and illegal interceptionConfidentiality
Data interferenceIntegrity
System interferenceAvailability

Law Is Only Part of the Picture

For IS students, knowing what the law prohibits is only one part of the analysis. As discussed in earlier topics, legal compliance is only one part of ethical responsibility. Cyberethics also considers questions that may not be fully resolved by law, including appropriate use of privileged access, reasonable security practices, proportionality of monitoring, and professional responsibility when vulnerabilities are discovered.

For example, an IT administrator may have legitimate access to a server for maintenance and discover that they can also access confidential files unrelated to their work. The fact that the system permits the access does not by itself provide a legitimate reason to use it.

This distinction is particularly important for IS professionals because their roles may give them capabilities and privileges that ordinary users do not have.

Technical capability is not the same as ethical permission.

09 · Cybercrime and Digital Harm

The Human Element

Cybersecurity is sometimes treated primarily as a technological problem, but people are involved at almost every stage.

Users choose passwords, respond to messages, handle information, configure systems, grant access, and make decisions about security. Attackers recognize this and often target human behavior rather than technical vulnerabilities.

Social engineering illustrates this clearly. A phishing message may impersonate a supervisor, colleague, bank, or trusted organization and create a sense of urgency. Even a technically secure system can be compromised if an attacker successfully persuades an authorized user to reveal credentials or approve access.

Beyond "the Weakest Link"

Describing people simply as the "weakest link" can oversimplify responsibility. Organizations also determine how systems are designed, how employees are trained, what safeguards are implemented, and how easy or difficult it is for users to behave securely.

If an employee falls for a sophisticated phishing attack, the employee's actions may need to be examined. But so should the surrounding system:

  • Did the organization provide adequate security awareness training?
  • Was multi-factor authentication available?
  • Could a single compromised account provide excessive access?
  • Was there an easy way to report suspicious messages?
Shared responsibility — Users have responsibilities to exercise reasonable care, while developers, administrators, managers, and organizations have responsibilities to create systems and environments that reduce both the likelihood and consequences of human error.

The ethical goal should not simply be to blame the person who made the mistake, but to understand why the failure occurred and how people and systems can work together to prevent similar harm.

10 · Professional and Organizational Responsibility

Information Systems Professionals Hold Positions of Trust

IS and IT professionals may have extraordinary access to organizational systems.

RoleTypical access or knowledge
System administratorsAccess to servers
Database administratorsPrivileges over large collections of information
DevelopersUnderstanding of weaknesses that ordinary users cannot see
Security personnelMonitoring of network activity
Technical staffEncounters with confidential information while performing their jobs

With this access comes professional responsibility. The ACM Code of Ethics requires computing professionals to respect privacy and honor confidentiality. It also emphasizes professional competence, careful evaluation of systems and their risks, and the design and implementation of systems that are robustly and usably secure.

Having privileged access therefore does not mean having unrestricted ethical permission to use it. Professionals are entrusted with capabilities because those capabilities are necessary for performing particular responsibilities.

Access and responsibility — The greater the access and control a professional possesses, the greater the importance of exercising that power responsibly.
11 · Professional and Organizational Responsibility

Security by Design

Security should not merely be added after a system has already been developed.

Security by design — considering security throughout the design, development, deployment, and operation of an information system. This includes decisions about authentication, authorization, data protection, logging, software updates, backups, recovery, and other safeguards appropriate to the system and its risks.
Design→ Development→ Deployment→ Operation

This reflects an important ethical principle:

Anticipate, don't wait.

Professionals should anticipate reasonably foreseeable risks rather than wait for harm to occur before acting.

For example, a developer building a system containing sensitive personal information should not wait until a breach occurs before considering access controls. Likewise, an organization operating a critical service should consider backups and recovery procedures before a major outage happens.

Security is therefore not solely the responsibility of a cybersecurity team. Developers, system administrators, database administrators, managers, vendors, users, and organizational leaders can all influence whether systems remain trustworthy and secure.

12 · Professional and Organizational Responsibility

Monitoring and Privileged Access

Organizations have legitimate reasons to monitor their systems. They may need to detect attacks, investigate suspicious activity, enforce security policies, and protect organizational resources.

But monitoring also creates power. A system administrator might be able to inspect logs containing user activity. A security team might monitor network traffic. An organization might track employee access to systems.

The ethical issue is not whether monitoring is always right or wrong. Instead, we should consider:

LensQuestion to ask
PurposeWhy is the monitoring being conducted?
ScopeWhat, and whom, does the monitoring cover?
NecessityIs the monitoring actually needed to achieve that purpose?
ProportionalityIs the extent of monitoring appropriate to the problem?
TransparencyDo people know that they are being monitored?

Monitoring that is appropriate for detecting a security breach may become ethically problematic if the same capability is used unnecessarily to observe employees' private activities. Similarly, privileged access provided for system administration should not be used for personal curiosity or purposes unrelated to one's professional responsibilities.

A recurring tension. Security measures themselves can create ethical risks if the power they provide is misused.

13 · Professional and Organizational Responsibility

Responding to Security Incidents

Ethical responsibility continues after a security failure occurs.

Suppose an organization discovers that attackers may have obtained users' personal information. The organization now faces several decisions:

  • How quickly should the incident be investigated?
  • Who needs to know?
  • What should affected individuals be told?
  • How can further harm be prevented?
  • Who should be held accountable?

Organizations may sometimes have incentives to minimize or conceal incidents to protect their reputation. But withholding important information may prevent affected people from protecting themselves.

Ethical incident response therefore involves balancing security, transparency, confidentiality, accountability, and prevention of further harm.

The responsibility of an organization is not simply to restore the affected system. It should also:

Understand what happened→ Limit the consequences→ Communicate with affected parties→ Prevent recurrence

14 · Professional and Organizational Responsibility

Discovering Vulnerabilities

IS professionals may discover vulnerabilities in systems, sometimes unintentionally.

Discovering a vulnerability does not automatically authorize someone to exploit it. Testing a system beyond one's authorization can itself create risks, expose confidential information, or disrupt services.

A responsible response requires considering:

Am I authorized to test this?→ Could further testing cause harm?→ Who should receive the report?→ How can it be fixed without increasing risk?

Discovering vs. exploiting

Discovering a weakness
  • Noticing that something is wrong — and reporting it responsibly
Exploiting a weakness
  • Using it, or testing it beyond one's authorization

Professional competence gives computing practitioners greater ability to affect digital systems. Ethical responsibility requires knowing when not to exercise that ability.

15 · Wrap-up

Key Takeaways

  1. Cybersecurity protects people, not just computers. It is about protecting the people, information, activities, and institutions that depend on digital systems.
  2. The CIA triad is a foundation. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability define what cybersecurity seeks to protect — and failures in each produce real harms, from privacy violations and financial losses to disruption of essential services.
  3. Organizations have a duty of care. Perfect security does not exist, but reasonable precautions against foreseeable harm are expected.
  4. Security is not the only value. Security measures should be necessary, proportionate, transparent, and respectful of the people affected.
  5. RA 10175 provides the Philippine legal framework for cybercrime. But professional responsibility extends beyond simply avoiding illegal behavior.
  6. Responsibility is often shared. Users must exercise reasonable care, while organizations must design systems and environments that reduce human error and its consequences.
  7. Privileged access is a position of trust. Access is granted for specific responsibilities, not for personal curiosity or unrelated purposes.
  8. Security should be built in by design. Professionals should anticipate foreseeable risks rather than wait for harm to occur.
  9. Ethical responsibility continues after an incident. Organizations should investigate, limit harm, communicate appropriately, and prevent recurrence.
  10. Technical capability is not the same as ethical permission. Discovering a weakness does not authorize exploiting it.
