Topic 6 — Software Development Ethics

A guided reading on the ethical responsibilities that run through the software lifecycle — quality, security, privacy, inclusive design and dark patterns, testing, maintenance, code reuse, documentation, professional codes, and how to reason through conflicting duties.

Faculty-in-Charge: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Introduction

Software Development as an Ethical Practice

Software is not neutral. The decisions made during its design, development, testing, deployment, and maintenance can affect people's privacy, security, access to services, opportunities, and even safety.

For information systems students and professionals, ethical responsibility therefore extends beyond making software that simply "works."

Software development involves a continuous series of choices. Developers decide what a system should do, what data it should collect, which users and situations it should accommodate, what risks are acceptable, and when a system is ready for deployment. These may appear to be purely technical decisions, but many have ethical consequences.

Scenario — A developer discovers a serious security vulnerability shortly before a scheduled system launch. Fixing it would delay deployment. Ignoring it would allow the project to meet its deadline, but users could be exposed to harm.

Two different questions

The technical question
  • Can the system be deployed?
The ethical question
  • Should it be deployed?

Professional responsibility requires developers to consider both. This is especially important because users often cannot independently evaluate the software they depend on. They trust developers, organizations, and technology professionals to exercise appropriate care on their behalf.

02 · Introduction

Why This Matters to Information Systems Students and Professionals

Information systems professionals may work as developers, analysts, project managers, database administrators, system administrators, cybersecurity professionals, consultants, or technology leaders. Even those who do not write code directly make decisions that shape software systems.

You may someday decide:

  • what requirements a system should have;
  • what information it should collect;
  • which risks are acceptable;
  • whether testing is sufficient;
  • whether a vulnerability must be fixed immediately; or
  • whether a system is ready for deployment.

Software development ethics therefore concerns more than programming. It concerns professional judgment throughout the lifecycle of an information system.

The central question

Not simply
  • Can we build it?
But also
  • Should we build it this way, and are we prepared to take responsibility for its consequences?

03 · Eight Dimensions of Responsible Development

Quality, Reliability, and Professional Responsibility

Responsible software development involves more than writing functional code. Ethical responsibility extends across the software lifecycle, from initial design decisions to deployment, maintenance, and eventual retirement. The next eight sections highlight areas where technical decisions can have important consequences for users, organizations, and society.

Developers have an ethical responsibility to produce software with reasonable levels of quality, reliability, and safety appropriate to its purpose. Users depend on software to behave as expected, often without having the technical knowledge to evaluate its quality themselves.

Software does not need to be perfect. Virtually all sufficiently complex systems contain defects. The ethical issue is whether the software has been developed according to an appropriate professional standard given its purpose, users, and potential consequences of failure.

Care Should Match the Potential Harm

A similar failure in…May result in…
A recreational applicationInconvenience
A financial, medical, transportation, or government information systemFinancial loss, incorrect decisions, disruption of essential services, or even physical harm

Professional responsibility also requires developers to work within their competence, follow appropriate development practices, and communicate significant technical limitations. Deadlines, limited budgets, and organizational pressure are real constraints, but they do not eliminate the developer's responsibility toward the people who depend on the system.

Key principle — Software quality is not only a technical objective. It is also an ethical responsibility toward the people who rely on the system.

Secure Software Development

Security is not merely something added after a system has been completed. Developers should consider security throughout the software development lifecycle.

Requirements→ Design→ Implementation→ Testing→ Deployment→ Maintenance

Poor development practices can introduce vulnerabilities through insecure authentication, improper access controls, inadequate input validation, exposed credentials, outdated dependencies, insecure configuration, or other weaknesses. Once deployed, these vulnerabilities can expose users and organizations to data breaches, fraud, service disruption, and other forms of harm.

Developer responsibilities
  • Following secure coding practices
  • Protecting credentials and sensitive information
  • Applying appropriate authentication and access controls
  • Keeping libraries and dependencies updated
  • Conducting security testing
  • Addressing known vulnerabilities
  • Designing systems with potential misuse and attacks in mind

Not every vulnerability can be anticipated or prevented. The ethical issue becomes particularly important when developers or organizations know about significant security risks but fail to respond appropriately.

This connects directly with cybersecurity ethics from Topic 4. A vulnerability may originate from a technical mistake, but knowingly leaving serious vulnerabilities unresolved can become an ethical issue because the consequences are often borne by users, customers, employees, or the public.

Key principle — Security is part of software quality and professional responsibility, not an optional feature added after development.

Privacy by Design

Software systems increasingly collect and process personal information. Developers therefore influence privacy through seemingly ordinary design decisions — what data is collected, how it is stored, who can access it, how long it is retained, and what happens to it afterward.

Consider — Should an application collect a user's location if the feature does not actually require it?

A system may technically be capable of collecting large amounts of information, but technical capability does not automatically justify collection. Collecting unnecessary data can create privacy risks even when there is no immediate misuse. Once information has been collected and stored, it can potentially be exposed, accessed improperly, or used for purposes users did not originally expect.

Questions for Privacy-Conscious Development

  • What personal information is genuinely necessary for the system to function?
  • Are users adequately informed about what is being collected and why?
  • How long should the information be retained?
  • Who should have access to it?
  • Is sensitive information adequately protected?
  • Can users exercise meaningful control over their information?
  • Can the same purpose be achieved with less personal data?
Privacy by design — considering privacy from the beginning of system development rather than addressing it only after the system has already been built. Privacy requirements can influence system architecture, database design, access controls, default settings, interfaces, and data-retention policies.

Developers should therefore think of privacy not merely as a policy statement or consent form, but as something that must be implemented through technical and design decisions.

The connection to Topic 3 is important: principles such as consent, data minimization, transparency, purpose limitation, and responsible data stewardship ultimately have to be translated into how actual information systems are designed and operated.

Privacy is a design responsibility.

Privacy protection is not only the responsibility of users or privacy officers. It is also a software design responsibility.

User-Centered, Inclusive, and Ethical Design

Developers make assumptions about who their users are and how they will interact with a system. Those assumptions can unintentionally exclude users or influence their choices.

A system might work well for developers during testing but create difficulties for people with disabilities, older devices, slow internet connections, limited digital literacy, or different linguistic and cultural backgrounds.

Does it work — and for whom?

Not only
  • Does the system work?
But also
  • For whom does it work, who might be excluded, and does the design respect users' choices?

Important Considerations

  • Accessibility and usability
  • Affordability and device requirements
  • Bandwidth limitations
  • Language and cultural context
  • Differing levels of digital literacy
  • Respect for user autonomy

Inclusive design does not mean that every system must accommodate every possible user. It means that developers should identify relevant users, recognize foreseeable barriers, and avoid unnecessary exclusion.

Ethical design also requires attention to how interfaces influence user decisions. Good interface design should help users understand their options and make informed choices rather than deliberately steering them toward decisions that primarily benefit the organization.

Dark Patterns and Manipulative Design

Interfaces that deliberately manipulate or obstruct user choices are commonly called dark patterns or deceptive design patterns.

PatternWhat it looks like
ObstructionMaking an action unnecessarily difficult, such as making cancellation much harder than subscription
SneakingHiding or delaying important information, charges, or consequences
Interface interferenceVisually emphasizing one choice while making alternatives difficult to find
Forced actionRequiring users to provide information or perform an unnecessary action to access a service
NaggingRepeatedly asking users to enable, subscribe to, or accept something they have already declined
False urgency or social proofUsing misleading countdowns, availability claims, or claims about other users to pressure decisions
ConfirmshamingUsing guilt-inducing language when users reject an option
Working as designed. Dark patterns are ethically significant because the software may be functioning exactly as designed. The problem is the design itself.

Developers may not always decide which interface strategies an organization adopts, but they may be asked to implement them. Professional responsibility includes recognizing and raising concerns about designs that undermine user autonomy.

The ethical question — Was the software designed to help users make an informed choice, or to manipulate them toward a particular one?

Testing, Quality Assurance, and Deployment

Testing and quality assurance help determine whether software actually meets its requirements and is ready for deployment. They are therefore not merely technical activities, but important forms of risk management.

Before deployment, developers should have reasonable confidence that the system performs as intended and that foreseeable failures have been considered. Ethical questions arise when teams discover defects, have incomplete testing, or face pressure to release software despite unresolved problems.

Scenario — A team discovers several defects before launch. None prevents the system from running, but one occasionally produces incorrect user records. Management insists that the launch date cannot be moved.

The ethical issue is not simply whether defects exist. Developers must consider:

  1. Severity — How serious is the defect?
  2. Likelihood — How often is it likely to occur?
  3. Consequences — What happens when it does?
  4. Affected stakeholders — Who bears the impact?

This is why practices such as quality assurance, user acceptance testing, staged deployment, monitoring, rollback procedures, and incident response matter ethically as well as technically.

Quality vs. testing

Quality
  • Describes the standard the software should meet
Testing & QA
  • Help determine whether that standard has actually been met before deployment

Maintenance and Software Sustainability

Software development does not end when a system is launched. Systems require ongoing maintenance throughout their useful life.

This may include updates, security patches, monitoring, documentation, performance improvements, dependency upgrades, and eventually replacement or retirement.

Technical debt — the result of short-term solutions that make future maintenance more difficult or costly.

Technical debt is not automatically unethical. Teams often make reasonable compromises because of limited time, resources, or changing requirements. It becomes ethically significant when organizations knowingly allow technical debt to create substantial risks while continuing to depend on the system.

Software sustainability therefore requires developers and organizations to consider not only whether a system works today, but whether it can be maintained safely and effectively over time.

Developers should communicate important technical limitations honestly. Hiding deteriorating systems, unsupported components, or serious maintenance problems prevents decision-makers from understanding the risks they are accepting.

Planning for Retirement

Eventually, systems may also need to be retired. Responsible maintenance therefore includes planning for:

Migration+ Preservation or disposal of data+ Continuity of essential services+ Impact on remaining users

Code Reuse and Intellectual Property

Modern software development rarely begins entirely from scratch. Developers routinely build on open-source software, frameworks, libraries, APIs, code repositories, sample code, and increasingly AI-generated code. Reusing existing code can save time, reduce duplication, and allow developers to benefit from the work of broader software communities.

However, the ability to access or copy code does not automatically mean that it can be used without restriction. Software is subject to intellectual property rights and licensing conditions, and different licenses may impose different requirements regarding use, modification, distribution, attribution, or sharing of derivative work.

Developers should consider
  • Software licenses and their conditions
  • Proper attribution where required
  • Compatibility between licenses
  • Ownership of proprietary source code
  • Security and reliability of third-party dependencies
  • Whether copied code is appropriate for the intended context
  • Whether reused or generated code can legally and ethically be incorporated into a project

For example, code posted publicly on a repository may still have licensing restrictions. Likewise, source code created for an employer or client should not simply be copied into another project without appropriate authorization.

Technical Responsibility for Reused Code

A third-party library may contain vulnerabilities, become unsupported, or behave differently from what developers expect. Developers should therefore understand important dependencies rather than treating external code as automatically trustworthy.

AI Coding Tools

AI-generated code may contain errors, vulnerabilities, or code resembling existing sources. Developers should not assume that generated code is automatically correct, secure, original, or appropriate for a particular project.

The developer remains responsible.

Regardless of whether code was written personally, copied from an external source, obtained from an open-source project, or generated with AI, the developer remains responsible for the code incorporated into the software.

Transparency and Documentation

Software development is collaborative. Other developers, administrators, managers, clients, and users may depend on information provided by the development team. Ethical professional practice therefore requires honest communication and appropriate documentation throughout the software lifecycle.

Developers Should Not Knowingly Misrepresent

AreaExamples of honest communication
System capabilitiesWhat the system can and cannot actually do
Testing resultsWhat was tested, and what was not
Security statusKnown vulnerabilities and their status
Project progressWhere the project really stands
Known defects & performance limitationsIssues users or maintainers should be aware of
Deployment risksRisks associated with going live

Documentation should also record important requirements, design decisions, testing results, changes, known limitations, and significant risks. This helps other professionals understand how the system works, why particular decisions were made, and what issues may require attention.

Good documentation is especially important when systems are maintained by people who were not involved in their original development. It supports continuity, informed decision-making, troubleshooting, maintenance, and ultimately accountability.

When something goes wrong, appropriate documentation can help answer an important question: Who knew what, what decisions were made, and what actions were taken?

12 · Professional Standards and Judgment

Professional Codes of Ethics

Ethical software development is not based only on personal judgment. Computing professionals also have professional standards that describe responsibilities toward users, employers, clients, colleagues, and society.

Two particularly relevant codes are the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and the Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice developed by the ACM and IEEE Computer Society.

ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

The ACM Code identifies broad ethical principles for computing professionals. Among those especially relevant to software development are responsibilities to:

  • contribute to society and human well-being;
  • avoid harm;
  • be honest and trustworthy;
  • respect privacy;
  • strive for high quality in professional work;
  • maintain professional competence;
  • evaluate systems and their risks thoroughly; and
  • design and implement systems that are robustly and usably secure.

These principles show that a developer's responsibility does not end when code executes correctly. Developers should also consider whether the software is sufficiently reliable, secure, usable, and appropriate for the people affected by it.

Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice

The Software Engineering Code organizes professional responsibilities around eight principles:

  1. Public — Act consistently with the public interest.
  2. Client and Employer — Serve the legitimate interests of clients and employers while remaining consistent with the public interest.
  3. Product — Ensure that software products and related modifications meet the highest professional standards possible.
  4. Judgment — Maintain integrity and independence in professional judgment.
  5. Management — Promote ethical approaches to software development and maintenance.
  6. Profession — Advance the integrity and reputation of the profession.
  7. Colleagues — Be fair to and supportive of colleagues.
  8. Self — Continue learning and promote ethical professional practice.

These principles are important because software development involves multiple responsibilities at the same time. A developer has responsibilities to an employer or client, but also to users and the public. Meeting a deadline or following a manager's instruction does not automatically remove professional responsibility for the consequences of the software.

Beyond the person who pays

A software professional's responsibility is not limited to satisfying the person who requested or paid for the system.

When developers encounter unsafe systems, inadequate testing, security vulnerabilities, deceptive design, or other potentially harmful practices, professional ethics provides grounds for questioning, documenting, communicating, and addressing those concerns.

Professional Codes and Ethical Frameworks

Professional codes complement the four ethical frameworks discussed earlier in the course. Ethical frameworks provide different ways of reasoning about what should be done, while professional codes describe the standards and responsibilities expected within the computing profession. Together, they help software professionals move beyond personal opinion when confronting difficult development decisions.

13 · Professional Standards and Judgment

Applying the Four Ethical Frameworks

Ethical frameworks can help software professionals examine difficult development decisions systematically. Consider this situation:

Case: The banking app update — A development team is preparing to release an update to a mobile banking application. During final testing, they discover that under certain conditions, completed fund transfers are not immediately reflected in the user's transaction history. The money is transferred correctly, but the application may temporarily show outdated information. Management wants to release the update on schedule and fix the issue in a later patch.

Consequentialism

Consequentialism focuses on the likely outcomes of the decision. Releasing the update on time may benefit the company and allow users to access new features. However, inaccurate transaction information could cause confusion, duplicate transfers, financial mistakes, customer complaints, or loss of trust. The team should consider the likelihood and severity of the potential benefits and harms associated with releasing or delaying the software.

Deontology

Deontology focuses on duties and obligations. Software developers have responsibilities to provide accurate information, communicate known limitations honestly, protect users, and avoid knowingly exposing them to preventable risks. The question is not only whether serious harm is likely; the team must also consider whether knowingly releasing software with the identified defect would violate its professional duties toward users.

Virtue Ethics

Virtue ethics asks what a good and responsible professional would do. Qualities such as honesty, competence, prudence, responsibility, and courage become important. Developers should disclose the problem rather than conceal or minimize it, and be willing to raise concerns when professional judgment conflicts with pressure to meet a deadline. The focus is on the kind of professional the developer chooses to be, not simply on compliance with instructions.

Justice and Fairness

Justice and fairness examine how the benefits, risks, and burdens of a decision are distributed. The company may benefit from releasing the update on schedule, while users bear most of the consequences if the software provides misleading transaction information. Some users may also be more vulnerable to financial disruption than others. The team should ask whether it is fair for users to bear risks created primarily by organizational pressure to meet a release schedule.

Quick check

Ethical Frameworks as Lenses

FrameworkQuestion it directs attention to
ConsequentialismWhat benefits or harms could result from developing, deploying, or continuing to operate the software?
DeontologyWhat duties do developers have regarding software quality, security, privacy, transparency, and user protection?

The frameworks do not provide automatic answers. Rather, they help software professionals examine technical decisions from several ethical perspectives.

When Responsibilities Conflict

Software development ethics becomes most difficult when legitimate responsibilities conflict.

Common Tensions

On one side…On the other…
Meeting deadlinesConducting adequate testing
Satisfying clientsProtecting users
Reducing costsImproving accessibility
Collecting useful dataProtecting privacy
Releasing features quicklyAddressing security risks
Following management instructionsExercising professional judgment
Meeting legal requirementsConsidering broader ethical responsibilities

There may be no solution that perfectly satisfies every stakeholder. Developers may have responsibilities to employers, clients, users, colleagues, the profession, and the broader public, and these responsibilities may sometimes point in different directions.

Legal is not the same as ethical. Legal compliance is important, but it does not settle every ethical question. A system may comply with applicable laws while still collecting unnecessary information, using manipulative interfaces, excluding some users, or exposing people to avoidable risks. Likewise, an instruction from a manager or client does not automatically make a development decision ethically responsible.

Ethical judgment therefore requires developers to:

Identify competing responsibilities→ Consider harms & stakeholders→ Consult professional standards→ Communicate significant concerns

— rather than treating technical feasibility, organizational instructions, or legal compliance as the only considerations.

Quick check

15. A product owner says: "Our legal team confirmed the app complies with every applicable law, so there's nothing left to discuss ethically — including the countdown timers that claim 'Only 2 left!' when stock isn't actually limited." What is the best response?

Technical decisions have human consequences.

The responsibility of an information systems professional is not simply to create systems that function, but to exercise sound professional judgment about how those systems are designed, developed, deployed, maintained, and eventually retired.

15 · Wrap-up

Key Takeaways

  1. Software is not neutral. Development decisions affect people's privacy, security, access to services, opportunities, and even safety.
  2. "Can we build it?" is not enough. Professionals must also ask whether to build it this way and whether they are prepared to take responsibility for its consequences.
  3. Care should match the stakes. Software need not be perfect, but it should meet a professional standard appropriate to its purpose and potential for harm.
  4. Security and privacy are built in, not bolted on. Both must be considered throughout the lifecycle and implemented through technical and design decisions.
  5. Design shapes choices. Inclusive design avoids unnecessary exclusion, and dark patterns are unethical even when the software works exactly as designed.
  6. Testing is risk management. Defects should be judged by severity, likelihood, consequences, and who is affected.
  7. Responsibility outlasts launch. Maintenance, technical debt, and eventual retirement all carry ethical obligations.
  8. You own the code you ship. Whether written, copied, open-source, or AI-generated, the developer remains responsible for it.
  9. Honesty enables accountability. Developers should not misrepresent capabilities, testing, security, or progress, and should document key decisions and risks.
  10. Codes, frameworks, and judgment work together. Professional codes and the four ethical frameworks help navigate conflicting responsibilities — and legal compliance alone does not settle every ethical question.
