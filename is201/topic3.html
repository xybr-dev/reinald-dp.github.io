A guided reading on personal data, consent, surveillance, and the Data Privacy Act — and on copyright, digital ownership, and software licensing — with two cases that put the ethical frameworks to work.

01 · Introduction

Just Because We Can…

Digital technologies have transformed how information is created, collected, stored, analyzed, shared, and reproduced.

Everyday activities such as using a mobile application, accessing an online platform, searching the web, communicating through messaging services, or making an online purchase can generate significant amounts of data.

These capabilities create benefits, but they also create ethical responsibilities. Computing professionals routinely design and manage systems that collect information about people, monitor their activities, and provide access to digital resources and creations. Decisions that appear purely technical can therefore affect privacy, autonomy, security, ownership, and individual rights.

The central question

Just because technology allows us to collect, access, monitor, copy, or share something, does that mean we should?

02 · Introduction

Why This Matters to Information Systems Students and Professionals

Information systems professionals work at the intersection of people, organizations, data, and technology. They help design, develop, implement, and manage systems that collect information, support organizational processes, and influence how people interact with technology.

Decisions about what data to collect, who can access them, how long they are retained, and how users are monitored are therefore not merely technical decisions. They can affect privacy, autonomy, rights, security, and trust.

IS professionals also routinely work with software, datasets, digital content, and third-party resources. Understanding intellectual property, attribution, and software licensing is therefore an important part of responsible professional practice.

A well-designed information system should not only work correctly. It should also handle people, data, and digital resources responsibly.

03 · Privacy and Data Ethics

Privacy and Digital Privacy

What Is Privacy?

Privacy is sometimes understood simply as keeping information secret. However, privacy is broader than secrecy.

A person may willingly share information with one party while still expecting that it will not be disclosed to everyone else. A student, for example, may provide personal information to a university because it is necessary for enrollment. Providing that information does not mean that the student has surrendered all expectations about how it will be used.

Privacy therefore involves maintaining appropriate boundaries around ourselves and information about ourselves. In digital environments, these boundaries become more difficult to maintain because information can be collected, copied, combined, analyzed, and transmitted at enormous scale.

Digital privacy — the appropriate handling of personal information, communications, and activities in digital environments. It includes keeping information appropriately confidential and secure and maintaining reasonable control over how personal data are collected, processed, stored, and shared.
Quick check

2. A student gives her home address to the university registrar for enrollment. Later, she is upset to learn the address was shared with a private marketing firm. Why is this still a privacy issue even though she provided the information voluntarily?

Why Is Digital Privacy Different?

Privacy existed long before computers. Digital technologies, however, have transformed both the scale and nature of privacy concerns.

Consider an ordinary day. A person might use a smartphone to navigate to work, access email, search the web, purchase something online, communicate through messaging applications, watch videos, and interact with social media. These activities can generate information about the person's identity, interests, relationships, behavior, location, and preferences.

Modern digital environments make it possible to collect enormous amounts of information, combine data from different sources, transfer them almost instantly, and retain them for long periods. Digital privacy therefore raises questions such as:

  • What information is being collected?
  • Does the individual know that it is being collected?
  • Why is it being collected?
  • Who can access it?
  • How long will it be retained?
  • Can information collected for one purpose be used for another?
  • Can different pieces of information be combined to reveal something the person never explicitly disclosed?

A single piece of information may appear harmless, but combining many pieces of information can produce a detailed picture of a person's activities, preferences, relationships, or behavior.

Why Does Privacy Matter?

Privacy protects several important human interests:

InterestWhat privacy protects
AutonomySome control over information about ourselves and how we participate in the digital world
Protection from harmAgainst identity theft, fraud, harassment, discrimination, manipulation, reputational damage, and other harms from misused information
FreedomSpace for thought, communication, association, and expression without unnecessary intrusion — people may behave differently when they believe they are constantly observed
TrustThe relationship between individuals and the institutions that handle their data

For computing professionals, privacy is therefore not simply a technical feature. It is part of the relationship between those who design and operate technologies and those who use or are affected by them.

Privacy Is Not Absolute

Recognizing privacy as important does not mean that personal information should never be collected or accessed. Universities need student information to provide educational services. Banks require information to conduct financial transactions. Hospitals need medical information to provide treatment. Governments collect information to provide public services and may sometimes require access to information for legitimate public purposes.

The ethical question is therefore rarely "Privacy or no privacy?" A more useful question is:

The better question — When is collecting, accessing, or using personal information justified, and what limits should apply?

The existence of a legitimate purpose does not automatically justify unlimited collection or access. The degree of intrusion should be necessary, appropriate, and sufficiently justified.

04 · Privacy and Data Ethics

Personal Data, Data Protection, and Consent

What Is Personal Data?

Personal data — information relating to an identifiable individual.

Examples include names and contact information, identification numbers, financial information, health information, educational records, and biometric information. Digital systems can also generate or collect IP addresses, device information, location data, browsing behavior, transaction histories, and patterns of online activity.

Personal information therefore does not always look obviously private. Information can become sensitive because of how it is combined, analyzed, or used.

Data Privacy and Data Protection

These two terms are closely related, but they address different questions:

ConceptConcerned with
Data privacyWhether and how personal data should be collected, accessed, used, and shared
Data protectionThe safeguards that prevent unauthorized access, disclosure, loss, alteration, or misuse — access controls, authentication, encryption, backups, security policies, and other organizational and technical measures

Consider a university database containing student records. Suppose it uses strong encryption, multi-factor authentication, and carefully configured access controls. The data may be secure. Now suppose the university begins using those records for a completely unrelated purpose that students were never informed about. The database may remain secure, but the use of the data may still raise privacy and ethical concerns.

Security vs. privacy — Security asks whether data are adequately protected. Privacy also asks whether the data should be collected, accessed, or used in the first place.

Good cybersecurity contributes to privacy, but security alone does not guarantee responsible data practices.

Quick check

4. A company's customer database has never been breached and uses state-of-the-art encryption. Without telling customers, the company starts using their purchase histories to set individually higher prices. Which statement is most accurate?

Data Minimization

Modern computing systems make it easy to collect and retain enormous amounts of information. Organizations may therefore be tempted to collect information simply because it might become useful someday. But every additional piece of personal information creates additional responsibilities and risks.

Data minimization — collect only the information actually needed for a legitimate purpose.

For developers and system designers, this can begin with a simple question: Do we actually need this information? If the answer is no, perhaps it should not be collected.

Quick check

5. You're building a sign-up form for a campus library's e-newsletter. A teammate suggests also asking for birthdate, home address, and mobile number "in case we need them later." Applying data minimization, what should you do?

Consent

Consent means more than obtaining an affirmative response from a user. For consent to be meaningful, individuals should understand what information is being collected, why it is being collected, and how it will be used. Consent should represent a genuine and informed choice rather than simply a procedural step.

In digital environments, meaningful consent can be difficult to achieve. Users routinely encounter privacy policies, cookie notices, application permissions, terms of service, and consent forms. Many simply click Agree or Accept to continue using a service.

The consent question — Does clicking "I agree" necessarily mean that someone has made an informed and meaningful choice?

Imagine an application presenting a lengthy privacy policy written in highly technical language. The user must accept it to access a service that they genuinely need. The organization may have obtained a click. Whether the individual genuinely understood what they were agreeing to is a different question.

Consent therefore raises ethical questions about transparency, comprehension, choice, and the power relationship between the organization requesting data and the individual providing them.

05 · Privacy and Data Ethics

The Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012

In the Philippines, Republic Act No. 10173, or the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (DPA), provides the principal legal framework for protecting personal information. It establishes responsibilities for organizations that process personal data and rights for individuals whose information is processed.

Three general principles are particularly important:

  1. Transparency. Individuals should be appropriately informed about how their personal information is being processed.
  2. Legitimate purpose. Personal information should be processed only for declared, specified, and legitimate purposes.
  3. Proportionality. The collection and processing of personal information should be adequate, relevant, necessary, and not excessive in relation to the intended purpose.

The DPA also recognizes rights of data subjects, including rights relating to being informed, accessing and correcting personal information, objecting to certain processing, and seeking appropriate remedies concerning their data.

Consent is an important basis for processing personal information, but it is not the only lawful basis. The law permits processing under other specified circumstances, such as when necessary to fulfill a contract, comply with a legal obligation, protect vital interests, or satisfy other conditions provided by law.

06 · Privacy and Data Ethics

Surveillance, Tracking, and Profiling

Digital technologies do more than store information about people. They can also be used to observe activities, track behavior, and draw conclusions about individuals.

Websites can record pages visited and links clicked. Applications can track location and usage patterns. Online stores can record purchases. Employers can monitor organizational systems. Universities can maintain system logs. Governments and private organizations can use cameras and other technologies to monitor physical and digital environments.

Surveillance is not automatically unethical. A university may monitor its network for cybersecurity threats. A bank may monitor transactions for fraud. An organization may maintain access logs to investigate security incidents. The ethical problem is determining when legitimate monitoring becomes excessive intrusion.

Several questions are useful:

LensQuestion to ask
PurposeWhy is the monitoring being conducted?
NecessityIs monitoring actually needed to achieve that purpose?
ProportionalityIs the extent of monitoring appropriate to the problem?
TransparencyDo people know that they are being monitored?
AccessWho can see the information collected?
RetentionHow long will the information be kept?
Secondary useCould information collected for one purpose later be used for another?

Consider an employer who wants to ensure that employees are performing their work while working remotely. Recording login times may serve a legitimate administrative purpose. Continuously recording employees through their webcams would create a very different level of intrusion. Both involve monitoring, but their ethical implications are not the same.

Quick check

9. To check that work-from-home staff are productive, a manager wants to install software that takes webcam photos of employees every five minutes. Which lens most directly exposes the problem with this plan?

Profiling

Tracking becomes especially significant when collected information is combined to create a profile of an individual. A profile may contain information the individual directly provided, but it can also include inferences about interests, preferences, habits, characteristics, or likely behavior.

A person may never explicitly state that they are interested in a particular product or activity. Their browsing history, location, purchases, and online interactions may nevertheless allow a system to infer that interest. Profiling therefore raises additional questions:

  • What information is being used to construct the profile?
  • Does the individual know that profiling is taking place?
  • Are the conclusions accurate?
  • What decisions will be made using the profile?
  • Could inaccurate or inappropriate inferences harm the individual?

The ethical concern is no longer simply that information has been collected. The information is now being used to make judgments about a person.

Quick check

07 · Intellectual Property and Digital Ownership

Copyright and Intellectual Property

Privacy and data ethics concern information about people. Intellectual property and digital ownership concern the rights and responsibilities associated with things people create, own, use, and share.

Digital technology makes copying extraordinarily easy. A physical object generally cannot be given to another person while the original owner keeps the same object. A digital file, however, can be copied perfectly, rapidly, and at almost no cost. This creates enormous opportunities for education, creativity, collaboration, and innovation. It also creates ethical questions concerning ownership, attribution, access, and the rights of creators.

Intellectual property — legal rights associated with creations of the mind. Different forms protect different kinds of creations, including copyright, patents, trademarks, and trade secrets.

For computing and digital environments, copyright is particularly important because it applies to many forms of creative work, including text, images, music, video, and computer programs. Copyright gives creators and other rights holders certain rights over the reproduction, distribution, adaptation, and use of protected works.

There is an understandable ethical basis for protecting these rights. People invest time, effort, skill, and creativity in producing works and have legitimate interests in how those works are used. At the same time, society benefits when knowledge, culture, software, and creative works can circulate. Education depends heavily on access to information, while innovation often builds upon existing ideas and creations.

The recurring tension

How should we protect creators while also supporting access, learning, creativity, and innovation?

08 · Intellectual Property and Digital Ownership

The Philippine Intellectual Property Code

In the Philippines, Republic Act No. 8293, or the Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines, provides the principal legal framework for protecting intellectual property. It covers areas such as copyright, patents, trademarks, and other intellectual property rights.

For digital technologies, copyright is particularly relevant because it protects various forms of creative works, including literary and artistic works, computer programs, and other original intellectual creations.

Copyright gives creators and other rights holders certain rights over how protected works are reproduced, distributed, adapted, communicated, and otherwise used. However, these rights are not absolute. Philippine law also recognizes limitations and exceptions, including fair use, which allows certain uses of copyrighted works depending on the circumstances.

How the IP Code Protects Software

The Philippines takes a mixed approach to protecting software. Under RA 8293, a computer program is protected by copyright as a literary work, while some software-related inventions may also qualify for patent protection.

CopyrightPatent
What it coversThe expression of the software — its written code and how that code is structured and organizedA new technical solution to a problem, where software is part of how the problem is solved (e.g., a fraud-detection or disaster-warning system)
What it does not coverThe underlying ideas, methods, or abstract mathematical functionsAbstract algorithms or business methods on their own
When protection beginsAutomatically, from the moment the program is created — no registration requiredOnly after a formal application is examined and granted by IPOPHL, which can take years
Role of registrationOptional; recording the work with IPOPHL strengthens the owner's ability to enforce their rightsRequired

For developers, this means code is protected as soon as it is written — but so is everyone else's. The fact that a program carries no registration notice does not mean it is free to copy. And because copyright protects expression rather than ideas, developers may independently implement a similar idea or algorithm, but may not simply copy another person's code.

For computing professionals, the Intellectual Property Code establishes important legal boundaries when creating, copying, modifying, distributing, or incorporating digital materials into technological products and services. As with data privacy, however, legal compliance does not settle every ethical question.

Beyond the law — Respecting intellectual property involves not only asking what the law permits, but also what constitutes fair, responsible, and respectful use of another person's work.

09 · Intellectual Property and Digital Ownership

Digital Piracy and Fair Use

Digital technologies have made copying and distributing protected works extremely easy.

Digital piracy — unauthorized copying, downloading, distribution, or use of copyrighted material.

The ethical issue may initially appear simple: creators have rights, so unauthorized copying is wrong. Real situations, however, can involve competing considerations. Access to educational resources may be limited by cost, and some works may be difficult to obtain through legitimate channels. These circumstances may help explain why unauthorized copying occurs, but explaining a practice does not automatically justify it.

At the same time, copyright protection is not unlimited. Fair use recognizes that certain uses of copyrighted works may be permitted depending on the circumstances. Purposes such as criticism, comment, teaching, scholarship, research, and news reporting may be relevant — but simply labeling a use "educational" does not automatically make it fair use.

The determination of fair use depends on the circumstances, including:

  1. The purpose and character of the use
  2. The nature of the copyrighted work
  3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used
  4. The effect of the use on the potential market for the work

Ethical reasoning therefore requires consideration of both the rights of creators and the legitimate interests of users and society.

Authorship, Attribution, and Plagiarism

Copyright and plagiarism are related, but they are not identical.

ConceptPrimarily a…Concerned with
CopyrightLegal issueRights over protected works
PlagiarismEthical and academic issuePresenting another person's work, words, or ideas as one's own without appropriate acknowledgment

A student may take an idea from a freely available source and present it as their own. Even if copyright infringement is not the central issue, failing to acknowledge the source can still constitute plagiarism. Conversely, providing attribution does not automatically give someone permission to reproduce copyrighted material.

Two different questions

Attribution answers
  • Whose work is this?
Permission answers
  • Am I allowed to use it this way?

Computing professionals encounter similar responsibilities when reusing code, documentation, images, datasets, designs, and other digital materials.

11 · Intellectual Property and Digital Ownership

Ownership of Digital Content and Assets

Digital ownership can be less intuitive than ownership of physical objects.

When you buy…What you typically get
A physical bookOwnership of that particular copy — you can generally lend it, give it away, or resell it
A digital book, app, game, or movieOften a license to access or use the content under specified conditions

Digital platforms can impose restrictions on copying, transferring, modifying, or accessing digital content. This raises broader ethical questions:

  • What does it mean to "own" something digital?
  • What rights should users have over digital goods they have paid for?
  • Can access to purchased digital content be withdrawn?
  • What happens when a platform or service closes?
  • How should the interests of creators, platforms, and users be balanced?

Digital technologies therefore challenge familiar assumptions about ownership.

12 · Intellectual Property and Digital Ownership

Software Licensing and Open Source

Software is intellectual property, but obtaining software does not necessarily mean owning it. In most cases, users receive permission to use software according to a software license, which establishes what they may and may not do with it.

A license may specify whether software can be installed on multiple devices, copied or redistributed, modified, used commercially, or incorporated into another software product.

Owner-controlled

Proprietary Software

Generally gives the software owner greater control over its use, modification, and distribution. Users receive particular rights under the license rather than unrestricted ownership of the software.

Source available

Free and Open-Source Software (FOSS)

Takes a different approach. Its source code is made available under licenses that permit varying degrees of use, inspection, modification, and redistribution.

Heads up. Open source does not mean "no copyright" or "do whatever you want."

Open-source software remains subject to licensing conditions. Some licenses, such as the MIT and Apache licenses, generally permit broad reuse and modification provided that specified requirements are followed. Other licenses, such as the GNU General Public License (GPL), impose additional conditions when software is modified or redistributed.

This is especially important because modern software development relies heavily on existing frameworks, libraries, packages, plugins, and other third-party components. Finding code in a public repository does not automatically mean that it can be used for any purpose.

Quick check

15. A developer finds useful code in a public GitHub repository and says, "It's public, so I can drop it into our paid app however I like." What's the flaw in this reasoning?

Before incorporating third-party software, developers should consider:

What license applies?→ Does it permit the intended use?→ What obligations apply?→ Is attribution required?→ Can it be modified or redistributed?

For computing professionals, software licensing is therefore not merely a legal concern. It is also an issue of professional responsibility. Using someone else's code means respecting the conditions under which that work was made available.

Open source also raises a broader ethical question. An organization may legally benefit from community-developed software without being required to contribute anything in return. Responsible participation, however, might include reporting bugs, improving documentation, contributing fixes, or otherwise supporting the community.

Permitted vs. ought — What we are legally permitted to do does not always settle what we ought to do.

13 · Applying the Ethical Frameworks

Case 1: Student Data and Academic Analytics

Ethical frameworks become particularly useful when technological practices involve competing interests rather than an obvious right-or-wrong answer. The two cases that follow represent the major areas covered in this topic.

The case — A university develops a system that analyzes student activity in its learning management system (LMS). It examines login frequency, access to learning materials, submission patterns, quiz performance, and discussion participation to identify students who may be academically at risk. The intention is to identify struggling students earlier and provide appropriate support. However, students were not specifically informed that their behavioral data would be used for predictive analysis.
Consequentialist

Benefits vs. Harms

The system could enable earlier intervention and improve student outcomes. However, inaccurate predictions could misclassify students, create unnecessary interventions, or lead to inappropriate judgments. A student who rarely logs into the LMS, for example, may have downloaded materials for offline study because of unreliable internet access. The question is whether the expected benefits justify the potential harms and whether those harms can be minimized.

Rights-based

Privacy and Autonomy

Students have interests in privacy, autonomy, and appropriate use of information about them. Even if the university already possesses the data, students may reasonably ask whether they were adequately informed about this additional use and whether they can access or challenge conclusions drawn about them. The usefulness of the system does not automatically override individual rights.

Justice & fairness

Who Bears the Burden?

The system may not affect all students equally. Measures such as login frequency or time spent online could disadvantage students with unreliable internet access, demanding work schedules, disabilities, or other circumstances affecting how they interact with the LMS. A seemingly neutral system can therefore produce unfair outcomes.

Virtue ethics

What Would a Responsible Institution Do?

A responsible university and conscientious developer would not ask only whether the system can accurately predict academic risk. They would also consider whether the data are appropriate, whether students understand how they are being used, whether predictions can be challenged, and whether the system genuinely serves students.

14 · Applying the Ethical Frameworks

Case 2: Reusing Open-Source Code

The case — A developer finds an open-source library that could save several weeks of development. The developer incorporates it into the company's commercial software without checking the license. The code works successfully, and the product is released. The company later discovers that the license imposed conditions that were not followed.
Consequentialist

Short-Term Efficiency vs. Long-Term Consequences

Using the library saved time and resources and allowed the product to be completed sooner. However, disregarding the license can create legal and operational problems for the company, affect customers, harm the original developers, and undermine trust within the open-source community. Short-term efficiency must therefore be weighed against broader and longer-term consequences.

Rights-based

The Creators' Conditions

The original developers chose to make their software available under particular conditions. Public access to the source code does not eliminate their rights. Respecting those conditions recognizes the creators' legitimate authority over how their work is used and distributed.

Justice & fairness

Benefits Without Responsibilities

The company benefits commercially from work created by others while ignoring the conditions under which that work was shared. The fairness question is whether the company is receiving the benefits of open-source development while avoiding the responsibilities attached to those benefits.

Virtue ethics

Professional Integrity

A responsible developer should check the provenance and licensing of third-party code before incorporating it into a product. Professional integrity involves more than producing software that works. How the software was produced matters as well.

15 · Wrap-up

Key Takeaways

  1. Privacy is more than secrecy. It concerns appropriate boundaries, autonomy, freedom, and the responsible handling of information about individuals.
  2. Personal data create responsibilities. Organizations and computing professionals must consider why information is collected, whether it is necessary, how it is protected, and how it will be used.
  3. Consent should be meaningful. Agreement is ethically significant when people are appropriately informed and able to make a meaningful choice.
  4. The Data Privacy Act provides the Philippine legal framework for personal data protection. Its requirements are essential, but legal compliance does not eliminate the need for ethical judgment.
  5. Surveillance, tracking, and profiling are privacy issues. Monitoring can serve legitimate purposes, but its purpose, necessity, proportionality, transparency, and potential for misuse must be considered.
  6. Intellectual property rights continue to apply in digital environments. The ease with which digital works can be copied does not eliminate the rights and interests of creators.
  7. The Intellectual Property Code provides the Philippine legal framework for intellectual property. It protects creators and rights holders while also recognizing limitations and exceptions such as fair use.
  8. Copyright, attribution, and plagiarism involve different responsibilities. Attribution does not automatically grant permission, and copyright compliance does not necessarily prevent plagiarism.
  9. Digital ownership is not always equivalent to physical ownership. Purchasing digital content may provide access or usage rights rather than unrestricted ownership.
  10. Software licenses matter. Public availability or access to source code does not mean unrestricted permission to use, modify, or redistribute it.
  11. Legal compliance and ethical responsibility are not identical. Laws establish important requirements, but computing professionals must also consider fairness, harm, autonomy, responsibility, and the legitimate interests of others.

Just because we can, does it mean we should?
