IS 201 · Chapter 2 — Ethical Issues in Computing

Topic 5 — AI Ethics

A guided reading on how AI systems should be designed, developed, deployed, and used — from the shift toward a human-centered Fifth Industrial Revolution, to eight core ethical principles for AI, to applying them in real decisions where humans remain responsible and accountable.

Faculty-in-Charge: Dr. Reinald Adrian Pugoy — First Semester, AY 2026–2027
01 · Introduction

Can vs. Should

Artificial Intelligence is no longer confined to research laboratories or specialized computing applications.

AI systems increasingly influence how people communicate, learn, work, create, make decisions, access services, and interact with institutions. Generative AI has made this transformation even more visible by placing systems capable of producing text, images, audio, video, and computer code directly into the hands of ordinary users.

But the growing capability of AI also creates an important ethical question.

Just because AI can do something, does that mean it should?

AI ethics examines how AI systems should be designed, developed, deployed, and used so that technological progress remains consistent with human values, rights, responsibilities, and well-being.

02 · Introduction

Why This Matters to Information Systems Students and Professionals

AI is increasingly becoming part of the information systems used by organizations.

IS professionals may encounter AI in business applications, analytics, decision-support systems, automated workflows, customer services, and other digital platforms.

Even when IS professionals are not directly developing AI models, they may be involved in:

Selecting→ Implementing→ Integrating→ Managing→ Evaluating

The decisions made in these roles can affect organizations, users, and other stakeholders.

Understanding AI ethics is therefore part of responsible IS practice. As AI becomes more deeply embedded in information systems, IS students and professionals need to consider not only what these systems can do, but also how they should be designed, implemented, and used responsibly.

Remember — Technical capability alone does not determine responsible use.

03 · Understanding AI and Its Ethical Context

From the Fourth to the Fifth Industrial Revolution

The rise of AI can be understood within the broader transformation associated with the Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR).

Earlier industrial revolutions transformed society through mechanization, electricity and mass production, and later computers and digital technologies.

Mechanization→ Electricity & mass production→ Computers & digital tech→ 4IR: Convergence→ 5IR: Human-centered

The 4IR extends this transformation through the increasing convergence of digital, physical, and biological technologies. Artificial intelligence, big data, cloud computing, robotics, the Internet of Things, biotechnology, and other emerging technologies increasingly interact within connected and intelligent systems.

AI is particularly significant because it allows computer systems to perform tasks traditionally associated with aspects of human intelligence. In practical terms, AI systems can learn patterns from large amounts of data, classify information, make predictions, recommend actions, automate tasks, and generate new content.

AI is therefore converging with virtually every aspect of modern life. In research, for example, it can already assist throughout the research lifecycle, from ideation and literature review to analysis, writing, and dissemination.

But the 4IR also raises a deeper question: should technological progress be measured only by how much can be automated, optimized, predicted, or generated?

This concern leads to discussions surrounding the Fifth Industrial Revolution (5IR). Whereas the 4IR is strongly associated with technological convergence, intelligent systems, and automation, the 5IR emphasizes a more human-centered, sustainable, resilient, and socially responsible relationship with technology.

The emphasis begins to shift

4IR asks
  • What becomes possible when technologies become intelligent and interconnected?
5IR asks
  • How can these technologies serve people, society, and the environment responsibly?

This shift is crucial to computer ethics. Technological capability alone cannot determine whether a technology should be developed or used in a particular way. The central question therefore changes from "What can AI do?" to:

What should AI do, for whom, and toward what kind of society? — That is where AI ethics enters the discussion.

04 · Understanding AI and Its Ethical Context

Understanding AI Before Discussing AI Ethics

Responsible use begins with understanding the technology itself.

Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence (AI) — computer systems designed to perform tasks associated with human intelligence. AI systems typically learn patterns from large amounts of data and use those patterns to perform tasks such as classification, prediction, and generation.

This dependence on data is important. The behavior of an AI system is influenced not only by its algorithms but also by the data used to develop and train it.

Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative AI (GenAI) — a type of AI designed to create new content such as text, images, audio, video, or computer code. It learns patterns from large datasets and uses those patterns to generate new outputs based on what it has learned.

Large Language Models

Large Language Model (LLM) — a type of generative AI designed to produce human-like text.

At a simplified level, language models learn to predict likely continuations of text. Given an input, the model estimates what words or tokens are likely to come next based on patterns learned during training.

TermWhat it refers toExample output
AIBroad category: systems performing tasks associated with human intelligenceClassification, prediction, recommendation
Generative AIAI that creates new content from learned patternsText, images, audio, video, code
LLMGenerative AI that produces human-like textAnswers, summaries, essays, drafts
Fluency is not understanding. The fluency of an LLM should not be confused with human-like understanding. LLMs recognize patterns and generate responses using statistical relationships learned from enormous amounts of textual data.

Understanding this basic characteristic of AI is important when evaluating both its capabilities and its limitations.

05 · Understanding AI and Its Ethical Context

AI Brings Both Opportunities and Risks

AI should not automatically be treated as either beneficial or harmful.

Its ethical significance depends on how it is designed, what data it uses, how it is deployed, who controls it, and who is affected by it.

OpportunitiesRisks
Increase productivityProduce inaccurate information
Support learning and researchReproduce bias
Improve accessibilityExpose sensitive data
Analyze large datasets and assist decision-makingEnable misinformation
Automate repetitive tasksEncourage overreliance
Enable new forms of creativity and innovationContribute to other forms of harm

Powerful tools used without sufficient critical understanding can compromise accuracy, ethics, and integrity.

AI is therefore neither inherently ethical nor unethical. Ethical questions arise from the ways AI systems are designed, developed, deployed, governed, and used.

The central concern

How can AI be used responsibly and wisely?

06 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Responsibility and Accountability

Guide question: What responsibilities do people and institutions have, and who ultimately answers for the consequences?

Responsibility — the duties and obligations associated with the development, deployment, and use of AI.

Different actors may have different responsibilities:

ActorMay be responsible for…
DevelopersTesting systems and addressing known risks
OrganizationsEstablishing appropriate safeguards and policies
Professionals and usersEvaluating AI outputs before acting on them
Accountability — answerability. It asks who can be called upon to explain, justify, and take ownership of AI-related decisions, actions, and consequences.

Put simply

Responsibility asks
  • What are people and institutions expected to do?
Accountability asks
  • Who must answer for what was done and what resulted from it?

AI systems do not eliminate either responsibility or accountability.

Quick check

5. A bank's policy says loan officers must review AI credit scores before approving loans. After a wrongful denial, the bank president is asked by regulators to explain and justify what happened. Which pairing is correct?

Hallucination

This becomes especially important because generative AI can produce hallucinations.

Hallucination — AI-generated content that may be grammatically correct, authoritative in tone, and highly convincing while still being factually incorrect. AI systems may fabricate facts, references, quotations, explanations, or other information.

For example, an AI system may generate convincing academic citations that do not actually exist. If those references are used without verification, responsibility for verification remains with the person using them. If the resulting work is submitted, published, or used in making a decision, the relevant person or institution remains accountable for that use.

Similarly, reliance on an AI recommendation in making a consequential professional decision does not automatically transfer accountability to the AI system.

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence emphasizes responsibility and accountability throughout the AI lifecycle. Human or legal responsibility should remain attributable for AI-related decisions and actions.

AI assists. Humans remain accountable.

If humans remain responsible and accountable for AI-assisted actions and decisions, they must also retain a meaningful role in making those decisions.

07 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Human Judgment and Meaningful Human Control

Guide question: Who retains decision-making authority?

Accountability becomes difficult to defend if people are held answerable for decisions over which they exercise no meaningful control.

Responsible AI therefore requires more than simply placing a human somewhere in the process. Human involvement should be meaningful. People should retain genuine authority to:

Examine· Question· Intervene· Override· Reject

…AI-generated outputs and recommendations where appropriate.

The rubber-stamp problem. As AI systems become more capable and convenient, the risk of overreliance increases. A "human in the loop" provides little meaningful oversight if the human merely approves whatever an AI system recommends without critically examining it.

AI should augment human capability, not replace human judgment.

AI-generated outputs should not be accepted or reproduced uncritically. Critical thinking should not be outsourced to AI, and AI-generated outputs should not be assumed to be accurate or authoritative.

This principle applies across contexts:

WhoRetains responsibility for…
A teacherAcademic judgment
A physicianProfessional judgment
A managerOrganizational decisions
A studentLearning
A public institutionDecisions affecting citizens

UNESCO describes this principle as Human Oversight and Determination. AI may support human decision-making, but human autonomy and meaningful oversight should be preserved.

Meaningful human control also requires sufficient understanding of the technology being overseen.

08 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

AI Literacy and Competence

Guide question: What knowledge and competence are necessary to exercise that authority responsibly?

The widespread availability of AI creates an unusual situation. People can use highly sophisticated technologies without necessarily understanding how those technologies work, what their limitations are, or what risks their use may create.

Ease of use should not be confused with competence.

If humans are expected to exercise meaningful judgment and remain responsible and accountable for AI-assisted decisions, they need sufficient AI literacy and competence to do so. Responsible AI use requires understanding:

  • what AI can and cannot do, and how AI systems can fail;
  • hallucination and inaccuracy;
  • bias and discrimination;
  • effective and responsible prompting, and appropriate tool selection;
  • privacy and security implications;
  • verification of AI-generated outputs; and
  • broader social and environmental consequences.

Convenience without competence creates ethical risk.

Not just
  • Knowing how to operate an AI tool
But understanding
  • What the tool is doing, what its outputs mean, its limitations, the risks involved, and how outputs should be evaluated

UNESCO's principle of Awareness and Literacy similarly recognizes that people need sufficient knowledge and skills to make informed decisions about AI and participate meaningfully in societies increasingly shaped by it.

How the First Three Principles Connect

PrincipleWhat it establishes
ResponsibilityWhat people and institutions are expected to do
AccountabilityWho must answer for AI-related decisions and consequences
Meaningful human controlThat humans retain genuine decision-making authority
AI literacy & competenceThat informed exercise of that authority is possible

09 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Fairness and Non-Discrimination

Guide question: Are the benefits, risks, and outcomes of AI distributed fairly?

AI systems learn from data created within human societies. That data may contain existing social, cultural, economic, racial, gender, or other forms of bias. Consequently, AI can inherit these biases and sometimes amplify them.

Training data may also overrepresent some populations and underrepresent others. Large language models, for example, may reflect a Western orientation when their training data disproportionately represent Western or English-language sources.

Scenario — An AI-assisted hiring system is trained primarily on historical hiring decisions. If those historical decisions favored particular groups, the system may learn patterns that reproduce those inequalities. The system does not need to be deliberately programmed to discriminate for discriminatory outcomes to occur.

Several questions therefore become ethically important:

  1. Who is represented in the data?
  2. Who is missing?
  3. Who benefits from the system?
  4. Who may be disadvantaged?
  5. How can affected people challenge an unfair decision?

This principle takes its name from UNESCO's principle of Fairness and Non-Discrimination, which emphasizes that AI should not reinforce prejudice, discrimination, or existing inequalities.

Fairness also requires sensitivity to context. An AI system that performs well for one population, language, culture, or country may not necessarily perform equally well elsewhere.

10 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Transparency and Explainability

Guide question: Can the use and decisions of AI be understood and meaningfully questioned?

Responsible AI should not operate as an unquestionable black box.

Transparency — making relevant information about AI systems and their use visible and accessible. People affected by AI may need to know that AI is being used, what role it plays, what information it uses, how it contributes to a decision or output, and who is responsible for the system.
Explainability — goes further by asking whether people can meaningfully understand why an AI system produced a particular result.

This becomes especially important when AI affects consequential decisions involving employment, education, healthcare, finance, public services, or other important areas of life.

Consider a student who is denied a scholarship because an algorithm classifies the student as "high risk." Simply stating that the computer produced the decision would not provide a meaningful explanation. A person affected by an important automated decision should have some meaningful way of understanding, questioning, or challenging that decision.

UNESCO's principle of Transparency and Explainability recognizes these as important conditions for protecting human rights, enabling accountability, and sustaining public trust.

Understandable and contestable

Transparency is not merely about revealing technical details. It is also about making AI-mediated decisions understandable and contestable.

11 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Privacy and Data Protection

Guide question: Is data being collected, shared, and used responsibly?

AI systems depend heavily on data. This creates a direct connection between AI ethics and the ethical issues surrounding privacy and data discussed earlier in the course.

People may provide AI systems with personal information, student records, organizational documents, unpublished manuscripts, interview transcripts, photographs, confidential communications, proprietary information, or other sensitive data.

But not all information should be shared with AI.

Third-party AI risks. Using third-party AI systems may create risks of unintended data exposure, unauthorized processing, inappropriate secondary use, or loss of control over sensitive information.

Having access to data does not necessarily mean having ethical authority to disclose it. Before information is entered into an AI system, there should be a legitimate and ethical basis for sharing and processing that information.

UNESCO's principle of the Right to Privacy and Data Protection emphasizes that privacy and personal data should be protected throughout the AI system lifecycle.

Two requirements — Responsible AI requires both data protection and data judgment.

12 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Safety and Prevention of Harm

Guide question: What harms could result, and are the safeguards proportionate to the risks?

AI systems can create harm even when harm was not intended. An inaccurate medical recommendation, discriminatory hiring model, unsafe autonomous system, misleading deepfake, or incorrect AI-generated instruction can have consequences beyond the computer itself.

Responsible AI therefore requires anticipating possible harms before deploying a system. Important considerations include:

  • the likelihood and severity of harm;
  • who may be affected;
  • whether the system can be adequately tested;
  • whether humans can intervene when necessary; and
  • what happens when the system fails.

UNESCO addresses these concerns through two closely related principles:

UNESCO principleAsks…
Proportionality and Do No HarmIs the use of AI appropriate and proportionate to a legitimate objective, and can unnecessary harm be avoided?
Safety and SecurityAre unwanted harms and vulnerabilities prevented throughout the AI system lifecycle?

The degree of scrutiny and protection should therefore reflect the potential consequences of the system. An AI system recommending music does not require the same level of ethical scrutiny as an AI system influencing medical treatment, criminal justice, employment, or access to essential services.

The greater the potential harm, the stronger the safeguards should be.

13 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Sustainability

Guide question: Are the long-term social, economic, and environmental costs of AI justified?

AI may appear entirely digital, but AI systems depend on physical, economic, and social resources. Training and operating large AI systems require computing resources, data centers, electricity, cooling systems, hardware, infrastructure, and human labor. At scale, the development and use of AI can therefore have consequences that extend far beyond the immediate user or application.

Environmental impacts are particularly visible. Large-scale computing consumes energy and water and depends on physical infrastructure and hardware whose production and disposal also carry environmental costs.

But sustainability is broader than environmental impact alone.

Social+ Economic+ Cultural+ Environmental

UNESCO's principle of Sustainability encourages AI to be evaluated in relation to its wider social, economic, cultural, and environmental consequences. The question is not merely whether an AI system works today, but whether its development and use contribute to forms of technological progress that can be sustained without undermining human and planetary well-being.

Responsible AI therefore requires attention to broader societal and environmental impacts, including implications for labor, resource consumption, energy use, and long-term human and ecological well-being.

Remember — A technologically impressive solution is not necessarily a sustainable one.

14 · Applying AI Ethics

From Principles to Practice

Ethical principles are necessary, but principles alone do not automatically determine what should be done.

Scenario — A university wants to use AI to identify students who may be at risk of dropping out. Early identification might allow the university to provide academic assistance, counseling, or other interventions.

The system could provide significant benefits. But several ethical questions immediately arise:

PrincipleQuestion for the dropout-prediction system
Responsibility & AccountabilityWhat responsibilities does the university have when developing or using the system, and who must answer if it misclassifies a student or contributes to harm?
Meaningful Human ControlDoes the AI merely flag cases for human review, or does it automatically make decisions about students?
AI Literacy & CompetenceDo the people interpreting the prediction understand what the model can and cannot tell them?
Fairness & Non-DiscriminationDoes the model work fairly across different groups of students, or could its data and predictions disadvantage particular groups?
Transparency & ExplainabilityAre students aware that AI is being used, and can the university explain why a student was classified as "at risk"?
Privacy & Data ProtectionWhat student data are being collected and analyzed, and is their use appropriate?
Safety & Prevention of HarmCould an incorrect classification stigmatize students, affect how they are treated, or cause other harm?
SustainabilityAre the technological, social, economic, and environmental resources required justified by the expected benefits?

The same AI system can therefore appear beneficial under one ethical consideration and problematic under another. This is why ethical reasoning remains necessary.

Connecting AI Ethics to the Four Ethical Frameworks

The eight AI ethics principles can also be connected to the four ethical frameworks discussed earlier in the course. Each principle has a particularly strong conceptual connection to one of these frameworks.

Ethical FrameworkCore AI Ethics PrinciplesCentral Connection
ConsequentialismSafety and Prevention of Harm; SustainabilityFocuses on the consequences of AI, including immediate harms, risks, benefits, and long-term social and environmental impacts.
DeontologyResponsibility and Accountability; Human Judgment and Meaningful Human Control; Privacy and Data ProtectionFocuses on duties, obligations, rights, human autonomy, and answerability in the development and use of AI.
Virtue EthicsAI Literacy and CompetenceFocuses on the qualities needed for responsible AI practice, including competence, prudence, critical judgment, and intellectual responsibility.
Justice and FairnessFairness and Non-Discrimination; Transparency and ExplainabilityFocuses on equitable treatment and whether people affected by AI can understand, question, and challenge AI-mediated decisions.

These groupings represent the strongest conceptual connection between each AI ethics principle and the four ethical frameworks. They are not exclusive. The same principle may be examined through more than one ethical framework.

For example, privacy is strongly connected to deontology because it involves rights and duties, but privacy violations can also be examined through their consequences. Sustainability has a strong connection to consequentialism because of its concern with long-term impacts, but it can also raise questions of justice across populations and generations.

Likewise, AI-related problems such as hallucination, overreliance, bias, and privacy breaches are not themselves ethical frameworks or principles. They are risks or problems that can be examined through the eight AI ethics principles and, in turn, analyzed using the four ethical frameworks.

Principles and frameworks work together

The eight AI ethics principles
  • Identify what ethical concerns should be examined
The four ethical frameworks
  • Provide ways of reasoning about why those concerns matter and how they should be evaluated

AI ethics therefore does not replace the ethical frameworks studied earlier. Instead, it provides a technological context in which those frameworks can be applied to the particular challenges created by AI.

15 · Applying AI Ethics

The Central Ethical Question: Who Remains in Control?

AI ethics ultimately concerns more than the capabilities of machines. It concerns the choices made by people.

AI systems are developed by people, trained using data produced by people and societies, deployed by organizations, and used to make or support decisions affecting other people. Increasing technological capability therefore does not eliminate human responsibility or accountability.

Three ideas bring the discussion together:

  • AI assists. Humans remain accountable.— Responsibility and Accountability
  • AI should augment human capability, not replace human judgment.— Human Judgment and Meaningful Human Control
  • Convenience without competence creates ethical risk.— AI Literacy and Competence

Responsibility establishes the duties and obligations associated with AI. Accountability establishes who must answer for decisions and consequences. Meaningful human control preserves genuine human authority over consequential decisions. AI literacy and competence make the informed and responsible exercise of that authority possible.

These principles operate alongside broader ethical concerns involving fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, and sustainability.

The central ethical question

Not simply
  • What can AI do?
But
  • What should AI be allowed to do, what should remain distinctly human, and who remains responsible and accountable for the consequences?
16 · Wrap-up

Key Takeaways

  1. Capability is not permission. Just because AI can do something does not mean it should — AI ethics asks what AI should do, for whom, and toward what kind of society.
  2. The 5IR re-centers people. Beyond automation and convergence, technology should serve people, society, and the environment responsibly.
  3. Understand the technology first. AI learns patterns from data; LLMs predict likely text. Fluency is not understanding.
  4. AI is neither inherently ethical nor unethical. Ethical questions arise from how it is designed, developed, deployed, governed, and used.
  5. AI assists. Humans remain accountable. Hallucinated or AI-recommended content does not shift responsibility or accountability to the machine.
  6. Human control must be meaningful. A human who rubber-stamps AI outputs provides no real oversight; AI should augment, not replace, human judgment.
  7. Convenience without competence creates ethical risk. AI literacy means understanding limitations, risks, and how to verify outputs — not just how to use a tool.
  8. Fairness, transparency, and privacy matter. AI can inherit bias, must be understandable and contestable, and should only receive data there is a legitimate basis to share.
  9. Safeguards should scale with potential harm, and costs should be sustainable. Higher-stakes systems need stronger protections, and social, economic, and environmental costs must be justified.
  10. Principles identify concerns; frameworks help reason about them. The eight AI ethics principles connect to consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, and justice and fairness.
