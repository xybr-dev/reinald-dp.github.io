Topic 1 — Foundations of Computer Ethics

A guided reading on where ethics comes from, how it differs from morals and law, and the different ways scholars have defined what "computer ethics" even means.

01 · Foundations — Starting Point

What is Ethics?

Before we can talk about "computer" ethics, we need to agree on what ethics itself is asking.

Ethos — the word ethics comes from the Greek ethos, meaning custom or character.

Ethics is concerned with the rightness and wrongness of human actions. It asks fundamental questions such as:

  • How ought we to act?
  • What constitutes right conduct?
  • What makes a good life?

Ethics therefore goes beyond asking whether something is possible. It asks whether something ought to be done — a distinction that becomes very important once computers enter the picture.

Morals vs. Ethics — morals are an individual's personal principles and beliefs about right and wrong, while ethics refers to standards of behavior associated with a group, profession, organization, or society.

02 · Foundations — Distinctions

Ethics, Morals, and Law

These three concepts are often used loosely in everyday speech, but the reading draws a clear line between them:

ConceptBasic MeaningKey Question
MoralsPersonal beliefs and principles about right and wrongWhat do I believe is right?
EthicsStandards of acceptable conduct within a group, profession, or societyWhat ought we to do?
LawRules established and enforced by governmentWhat are we legally allowed or required to do?

These do not always agree. Something may be perfectly legal but still unethical. Laws provide important guidance, but they are not a complete guide to ethical behavior — a theme that will keep resurfacing throughout computer ethics, where technology often moves faster than the law does.

03 · Foundations — Character in Practice

Integrity and Ethical Dilemmas

Integrity means acting according to a personal code of principles and applying those principles consistently.

Ethical decisions are not always simple choices between right and wrong. Sometimes they involve an ethical dilemma, where important values or responsibilities conflict with one another.

Example: a software developer knows that a system still contains serious bugs, but is pressured to approve its release because the project is already behind schedule. The dilemma involves competing responsibilities — meeting organizational expectations versus ensuring that potentially harmful software is not released.

04 · Foundations — Bringing in Computing

What is Computer Ethics?

Computer ethics is concerned with ethical issues arising from the development and use of computers and information technology. It asks how people — particularly computing professionals — should make decisions concerning the professional and social consequences of computing.

As computing technologies have developed, different scholars have offered different ways of understanding what computer ethics actually is. That's the focus of the next section.

05 · Scope of Computer Ethics — Five Lenses

Perspectives on Computer Ethics

No single definition of computer ethics is universally agreed upon. Instead, several influential thinkers have each emphasized a different angle.

1970s

Walter Maner — New and Transformed Ethical Problems

Maner described computer ethics as the study of ethical problems that are aggravated, transformed, or created by computer technology. Computers may make existing ethical problems more serious, significantly change their nature, or even create ethical situations that would not exist without computing technology. Maner viewed computer ethics as a form of applied ethics, where traditional ethical theories are used to analyze problems involving computers.

Contrast with Maner

Deborah Johnson — New Forms of Familiar Problems

Johnson argued that computers often present new versions of familiar moral problems and dilemmas. Computing technology can make old problems more complex and place familiar moral principles in situations where their application is no longer obvious. Unlike Maner, Johnson did not generally view computers as creating entirely new moral problems — technology instead gives familiar ethical issues new forms and contexts.

Broader view

James Moor — Policy Vacuums and Conceptual Muddles

According to Moor, computers frequently create policy vacuums: technology makes new activities and choices possible, but society may not yet have appropriate policies governing how those capabilities should be used. Before appropriate policies can be developed, we may need to resolve conceptual muddles — new technologies can challenge existing concepts and force us to clarify what they mean in new contexts. Moor also described computers as logically malleable, shaped and programmed to perform an enormous variety of activities, which lets computing become deeply integrated into work, education, money, privacy, and other areas of life.

Values-centered

Computer Ethics and Human Values

Another approach examines how information technology affects important human values, including health, wealth, opportunity, freedom, democracy, knowledge, privacy, security, and self-fulfillment. From this view, computer ethics is concerned with identifying and analyzing the effects of information technology on these values.

Professional lens

Donald Gotterbarn — Computer Ethics as Professional Ethics

Gotterbarn emphasized computer ethics as a form of professional ethics — the values, standards of practice, and codes of conduct that guide computing professionals in their work. Professional responsibility extends beyond simply making technology function correctly; computing professionals must also consider the ethical implications of the systems they help create.

06 · Scope of Computer Ethics — Broader Landscape

Major Topics in Computer Ethics

Computer ethics covers a wide range of issues arising from the use of computers and information technology — concerning not only the technology itself, but also its effects on individuals, professionals, organizations, and society.

Labor & Automation

1. Computers in the Workplace

Computers can significantly change the nature of work and employment. Because computers can perform many tasks efficiently, they may replace some forms of human labor — while also creating new occupations and increasing demand for new technical skills. Even when jobs are not eliminated, technology may substantially change them. Automation can lead to de-skilling, where workers become less involved in performing tasks and instead monitor or operate automated systems, though it can also create jobs requiring more sophisticated skills. The introduction of computers into the workplace also raises concerns about worker health, safety, stress, and job satisfaction.

Key ethical question — How should the benefits of automation be balanced against its effects on workers?
Security & Misuse

2. Computer Crime

Computer ethics also deals with the security of computer systems and the misuse of computing resources. An important concern is logical security, which includes privacy and confidentiality; integrity of data and programs; unimpaired service; consistency of data and system behavior; and control of access to resources. Threats can come from malicious software such as viruses, worms, Trojan horses, logic bombs, and other programs designed to disrupt or damage systems. Security problems are not limited to outsiders — computer crimes may also be committed by trusted users who already have authorized access to a system. Unauthorized access or hacking also raises ethical issues: even when someone enters a system without intending to cause damage, the unauthorized access itself can force the owner to investigate whether information or systems have been compromised.

Key ethical question — When does the use of computing knowledge or access become an abuse of that capability?

Personal Information

3. Privacy and Anonymity

Computers make it possible to collect, store, search, compare, retrieve, analyze, and share large amounts of personal information efficiently. These capabilities have made privacy one of the longstanding concerns of computer ethics — particularly when dealing with sensitive personal information. Computer technology has also encouraged debate about what privacy actually means: one view defines privacy in terms of a person's control over personal information, while another argues that privacy is better understood in terms of restricted access to information. Privacy can also exist in public contexts, leading to the idea of privacy in public. Closely related is anonymity, which can protect people discussing sensitive matters and provide benefits similar to privacy — though both privacy and anonymity can also be exploited to facilitate undesirable or harmful activities.

Key ethical question — How should privacy and anonymity be protected while recognizing that they can also be misused?

Ownership & Access

4. Intellectual Property

Computer ethics examines questions about the ownership and use of software and other intellectual creations. One debate concerns whether software should be freely available for people to copy, study, and modify, or whether developers should be able to control its use and receive compensation for their work. Software ownership can involve different elements, including source code, object code, algorithms, and the "look and feel" of a program. Different forms of legal protection may also apply, including copyrights, trade secrets, and patents. These issues create tension between protecting creators and encouraging access, innovation, competition, and the sharing of knowledge.

Key ethical question — How should the rights of creators be balanced against the interests of users and society?

Codes of Conduct

5. Professional Responsibility

Computing professionals possess specialized knowledge and capabilities that can significantly affect other people and society. With this capability comes a responsibility to exercise professional power appropriately. Computing professionals may hold several kinds of relationships at once — employer and employee, client and professional, professional and professional, and society and professional — and these relationships can involve competing interests and potential conflicts of interest. Professional organizations such as the ACM and IEEE have developed codes of ethics to help computing professionals understand their responsibilities, including principles such as avoiding harm, being honest and trustworthy, maintaining professional competence, respecting applicable laws, and avoiding conflicts of interest.

Central principle — professional knowledge and authority come with professional responsibility.
Global Information Ethics

6. Globalization

Computer networks connect people and organizations across geographic, cultural, political, and economic boundaries. As a result, computer ethics increasingly becomes a form of global information ethics. Information technology creates situations in which ethical standards and human values must be considered across societies that may have different cultures, laws, traditions, and beliefs — raising the challenge that ethical standards for global technologies cannot always be understood solely within the context of one country or culture.

Key ethical question — How can appropriate standards of conduct be developed for technologies that operate across different societies and cultures?

07 · Standards & Practice — Formalizing Standards

Code of Ethics

A code of ethics identifies important ethical issues and establishes the overarching values and principles that should guide members of an organization or profession. A good code can help people:

  • Recognize ethical issues
  • Guide their decisions
  • Establish expectations for appropriate conduct
  • Provide mechanisms for reporting unethical behavior
  • Promote honesty and accountability

Having a code, however, is not sufficient by itself. Ethical standards must also be supported and practiced by the people and leadership of the organization. For computing professionals, professional codes of ethics provide guidance whenever technical decisions also carry ethical weight.

08 · Standards & Practice — A Practical Code

Ten Commandments of Computer Ethics

The Computer Ethics Institute proposed the following as practical principles for responsible computer use:

  1. Do not use a computer to harm other people.
  2. Do not interfere with other people's computer work.
  3. Do not snoop around in other people's computer files.
  4. Do not use a computer to steal.
  5. Do not use a computer to bear false witness.
  6. Do not copy or use proprietary software without paying for it.
  7. Do not use other people's computer resources without authorization or proper compensation.
  8. Do not appropriate other people's intellectual output.
  9. Think about the social consequences of the programs and systems you create.
  10. Always use computers with consideration and respect for other people.

These principles emphasize that responsible computing involves not only the proper use of technology, but also respect for other people and consideration of the consequences of one's actions.

09 · Standards & Practice — Putting It Into Practice

Ethical Decision Making

Ethical issues often require a systematic process rather than an immediate judgment. A basic decision-making process looks like this:

Define the problem→ Identify alternatives→ Choose an alternative→ Implement the decision→ Evaluate the results

Ethical considerations can be incorporated by identifying the people or groups affected by the decision, examining applicable laws, policies, professional standards, and ethical principles, and considering the possible consequences of each alternative. A useful set of guiding questions:

  • What is the ethical problem?
  • Who will be affected?
  • What values or principles are involved?
  • What laws, policies, or professional standards apply?
  • What are the possible consequences?
  • What is the most responsible course of action?
10 · Wrap-Up — Closing Reflection

A Closing Reflection

Computer ethics provides principles, professional standards, and frameworks for thinking about what ought to be done. Ultimately, ethical conduct is reflected in how we exercise our responsibilities and how our decisions affect others.

  • "He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?"— Micah 6:8 (KJV)

The verse offers three simple principles that can also guide ethical reflection in computing:

  • Do justly. Consider what is right and how technological decisions affect others.
  • Love mercy. Remember that behind data, systems, and technologies are people who may be helped or harmed by our decisions.
  • Walk humbly. Recognize the limits of our knowledge and exercise our capabilities and professional responsibilities with care.

In computer ethics, the question is therefore not only "What can technology do?" but also "What ought we to do?"

11 · Wrap-Up — Takeaway

Key Idea

Technical capability alone does not determine what should be done.

We usually ask
  • Can we do it?
  • Does it work?
  • Is it legal?
Computer ethics also asks
  • Should we do it?
  • Who will be affected?
  • What values are at stake?
  • What responsibilities do we have?
